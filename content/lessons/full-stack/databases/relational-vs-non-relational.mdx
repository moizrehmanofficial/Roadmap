Learn from zero how relational databases (PostgreSQL, MySQL) store data in linked tables with a fixed schema, foreign keys and constraints, and how non-relational (NoSQL) databases store documents, key-value pairs, wide columns or graphs. Model the same order in PostgreSQL and MongoDB, see what each makes easy (joins and enforced rules vs one-read documents and flexible shape), what each makes hard, and how to choose. All verified with PostgreSQL 16 and MongoDB 8."

<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
A **relational database** (PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, SQLite) stores data in **tables** with fixed **columns**, and connects tables with **keys**: an order row stores `customer_id`, which points to a row in `customers`. Each fact lives in **one place**, you combine tables at read time with **JOINs**, and the database **enforces rules** you declare (types, `NOT NULL`, `UNIQUE`, `CHECK`, foreign keys) plus **transactions** that keep related changes together. You talk to it in **SQL**. A **non-relational (NoSQL)** database uses another model: **documents** (MongoDB stores JSON-like objects with nested data), **key-value** (Redis: a key and a value, extremely fast), **wide-column** (Cassandra: huge write volumes spread across many servers) or **graph** (Neo4j: relationships first). Documents let you read a whole order with its items in **one read** and let records differ in shape, but data you copy into documents must be **updated in every copy**, and the database doesn't enforce rules unless you add them. Neither family is "better": **relational is the safe default** for most business data; pick NoSQL when its model clearly fits your access pattern or scale. Many systems use both.
</En>
<Ur>
**Relational database** (PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, SQLite) data ko muqarrar **columns** wale **tables** me rakhta hai, aur tables ko **keys** se jorta hai: order ki row `customer_id` rakhti hai, jo `customers` ki ek row ki taraf ishara karta hai. Har haqeeqat **ek jagah** rehti hai, aap parhte waqt tables ko **JOINs** se milate hain, aur database aap ke bayan kiye **qawaid nafiz** karta hai (types, `NOT NULL`, `UNIQUE`, `CHECK`, foreign keys) plus **transactions** jo mutaliqa tabdeeliyan ikatthi rakhti hain. Is se aap **SQL** me baat karte hain. **Non-relational (NoSQL)** database koi aur model istemal karta hai: **documents** (MongoDB nested data wale JSON jaise objects rakhta hai), **key-value** (Redis: ek key aur ek value, behad tez), **wide-column** (Cassandra: bohot se servers par phaile bhari writes) ya **graph** (Neo4j: pehle rishte). Documents aap ko poora order us ke items ke saath **ek read** me parhne dete hain aur records ki shakal mukhtalif hone dete hain, magar documents me copy kiya data **har copy me update** karna parta hai, aur database qawaid nafiz nahi karta jab tak aap na jorein. Koi khandan "behtar" nahi: zyada tar business data ke liye **relational mehfooz default** hai; NoSQL tab chunein jab us ka model aap ke access pattern ya scale par wazeh tor par fit ho. Bohot se systems dono istemal karte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>
</BigIdea>

<Why>
<Tr>
<En>

- The database is the hardest part of a system to change later; the model you pick shapes every query and every bug.
- Relational rules stop whole classes of bugs (orders for customers that don't exist, negative quantities) at the database.
- Document models can make common reads simpler and faster, and some workloads only scale with a specific NoSQL design.

</En>

- Database system ka wo hissa hai jise baad me badalna sab se mushkil hai; chuna hua model har query aur har bug ki shakal banata hai.

- Document models aam reads ko saada aur tez bana sakte hain, aur kuch kaam sirf makhsoos NoSQL design se scale hote hain.

## Picture it

<Tr>
<En>

Think of two ways to keep a **shop's paperwork**.

- **Relational** is a set of **ledgers**: one book of customers, one of products, one of orders, each line pointing to entries in the other books by number. Change a product's name once in the product book and every order that mentions product #1 shows the new name. A strict **clerk** refuses entries that point to customer #99 who doesn't exist. Answering "what did Jane buy?" means flipping between books (JOINs).
- **Documents** are a **folder per order**: each folder contains a photocopy of the customer's details and the product names and prices on that day. Grab one folder and you have everything, no flipping. But if a product is renamed, you must update every folder that has a copy, and nobody checks what you put in a folder unless you hire someone to (schema validation).

</En>
<Ur>

**Dukan ke kaghazat** rakhne ke do tareeqon ka sochein.

- **Relational** **khaata kitabon** ka set hai: customers ki ek kitab, products ki ek, orders ki ek, har line doosri kitabon ke entries ki taraf number se ishara karti hai. Product kitab me product ka naam ek dafa badlein aur product #1 ka zikr karne wala har order naya naam dikhata hai. Sakht **munshi** aisi entries rad karta hai jo na mojood customer #99 ki taraf ishara karen. "Jane ne kya khareeda?" ka jawab kitabon ke beech palatna hai (JOINs).
- **Documents** **har order ka ek folder** hain: har folder me customer ki tafseel aur us din ke product naamon aur qeematon ki photocopy hai. Ek folder uthayen aur sab kuch mil gaya, palatna nahi. Magar product ka naam badle to har wo folder update karna parta hai jis me copy hai, aur folder me kya rakha koi check nahi karta jab tak aap kisi ko rakhen (schema validation).

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Learn it step by step

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="The relational model in PostgreSQL">

<Tr>
<En>
Four tables: `customers`, `products`, `orders` and `order_items`, linked by foreign keys and protected by constraints (`NOT NULL`, `UNIQUE`, `CHECK`). Jane orders two lamps and a monitor arm. We read the order with a JOIN, try four kinds of bad data, and rename a product.
</En>
<Ur>
Chaar tables: `customers`, `products`, `orders` aur `order_items`, foreign keys se jure aur constraints (`NOT NULL`, `UNIQUE`, `CHECK`) se mehfooz. Jane do lamps aur ek monitor arm order karti hai. Hum JOIN se order parhte hain, chaar qism ka ghalat data azmate hain, aur ek product ka naam badalte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
export PGPASSWORD=postgres
psql -q -h localhost -U postgres -c 'DROP DATABASE IF EXISTS shop' -c 'CREATE DATABASE shop' 2> /dev/null
cat > schema.sql <<'EOF'
-- Relational: each kind of thing in its own table, linked by keys
CREATE TABLE customers (
  id    serial PRIMARY KEY,
  name  text NOT NULL,
  email text NOT NULL UNIQUE
);
CREATE TABLE products (
  id          serial PRIMARY KEY,
  name        text NOT NULL,
  price_cents int  NOT NULL CHECK (price_cents > 0)
);
CREATE TABLE orders (
  id          serial PRIMARY KEY,
  customer_id int NOT NULL REFERENCES customers(id),
  created_at  timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);
CREATE TABLE order_items (
  order_id   int NOT NULL REFERENCES orders(id),
  product_id int NOT NULL REFERENCES products(id),
  qty        int NOT NULL CHECK (qty > 0),
  PRIMARY KEY (order_id, product_id)
);
INSERT INTO customers (name, email) VALUES ('Jane Doe', 'jane@example.com');
INSERT INTO products (name, price_cents) VALUES ('Desk Lamp', 2999), ('Monitor Arm', 5400);
INSERT INTO orders (customer_id) VALUES (1);
INSERT INTO order_items VALUES (1, 1, 2), (1, 2, 1);
EOF
q() { psql -h localhost -U postgres -d shop -v ON_ERROR_STOP=0 -c "$1" 2>&1 | sed '/^$/d'; }
psql -q -h localhost -U postgres -d shop -f schema.sql
echo "--- one order = a JOIN across 4 tables"
q "SELECT o.id AS order_id, c.name AS customer, p.name AS product, i.qty, p.price_cents * i.qty AS line_cents
   FROM orders o JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
   JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.id JOIN products p ON p.id = i.product_id ORDER BY p.id;"
echo "--- the database refuses bad data"
q "INSERT INTO orders (customer_id) VALUES (99);"                 | grep ERROR
q "INSERT INTO order_items VALUES (1, 1, 0);"                       | grep ERROR
q "INSERT INTO customers (name, email) VALUES ('Jane 2', 'jane@example.com');" | grep ERROR
q "DELETE FROM products WHERE id = 1;"                              | grep ERROR
echo "--- one fact lives in one place: rename a product once"
q "UPDATE products SET name = 'LED Desk Lamp' WHERE id = 1;" > /dev/null
q "SELECT p.name, i.qty FROM order_items i JOIN products p ON p.id = i.product_id WHERE i.order_id = 1 ORDER BY p.id;"
```

```text output
--- one order = a JOIN across 4 tables
 order_id | customer |   product   | qty | line_cents 
----------+----------+-------------+-----+------------
        1 | Jane Doe | Desk Lamp   |   2 |       5998
        1 | Jane Doe | Monitor Arm |   1 |       5400
(2 rows)
--- the database refuses bad data
ERROR:  insert or update on table "orders" violates foreign key constraint "orders_customer_id_fkey"
ERROR:  new row for relation "order_items" violates check constraint "order_items_qty_check"
ERROR:  duplicate key value violates unique constraint "customers_email_key"
ERROR:  update or delete on table "products" violates foreign key constraint "order_items_product_id_fkey" on table "order_items"
--- one fact lives in one place: rename a product once
     name      | qty 
---------------+-----
 LED Desk Lamp |   2
 Monitor Arm   |   1
(2 rows)
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- Reading one complete order took a **JOIN across four tables**: each piece of information was stored once, in its own table, and combined at read time.
- The database **refused** an order for customer 99 (foreign key), a quantity of 0 (`CHECK`), a second account with the same email (`UNIQUE`), and deleting a product that existing orders still use. These rules hold no matter which app, script or developer writes to the database.
- Renaming the product **once** changed it everywhere it's used, because orders store only `product_id`. There's no copy to forget.
- The cost: more tables to design, and queries that combine them. With indexes on the keys, JOINs like this are very fast even on millions of rows.

</En>
<Ur>

- Ek mukammal order parhne ke liye **chaar tables ka JOIN** laga: har maloomat ek dafa, apne table me rakhi gayi, aur parhte waqt milayi gayi.
- Database ne customer 99 ka order (foreign key), 0 ki miqdar (`CHECK`), ek hi email ka doosra account (`UNIQUE`), aur aisa product mitana jo mojooda orders ab bhi istemal karte hain **rad** kiya. Ye qawaid qaim rehte hain chahe koi bhi app, script ya developer database me likhe.
- Product ka naam **ek dafa** badalne se har jagah badal gaya jahan istemal hota hai, kyunke orders sirf `product_id` rakhte hain. Bhoolne ke liye koi copy nahi.
- Kharcha: design karne ko zyada tables, aur unhen milane wali queries. Keys par indexes ke saath aise JOINs lakhon rows par bhi bohot tez hote hain.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="The document model in MongoDB">

<Tr>
<En>
The same order as **one MongoDB document** (MongoDB runs in a Docker container): the customer's details and each item's name and price are embedded inside. We read it in one go, store a second order with an extra field, save a clearly broken document, add a `$jsonSchema` validator and try again, then rename the lamp.
</En>
<Ur>
Wahi order **ek MongoDB document** ke tor par (MongoDB Docker container me chalta hai): customer ki tafseel aur har item ka naam aur qeemat andar embed hain. Hum ise ek dafa me parhte hain, izafi field wala doosra order rakhte hain, wazeh tor par toota document save karte hain, `$jsonSchema` validator jor kar dobara koshish karte hain, phir lamp ka naam badalte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
docker rm -f mongo-demo > /dev/null 2>&1
docker run -d --name mongo-demo -p 27017:27017 mongo:8 > /dev/null
npm init -y > /dev/null && npm pkg set type=module && npm install mongodb@7.9.1 --silent
cat > docs.js <<'EOF'
import { MongoClient } from "mongodb";

const client = new MongoClient("mongodb://localhost:27017");
await client.connect();
const db = client.db("shop");
await db.dropDatabase();
const orders = db.collection("orders");

// Document model: one order is ONE document, with its items (and a copy of what it needs) inside
await orders.insertOne({
  _id: 1,
  customer: { name: "Jane Doe", email: "jane@example.com" },
  items: [
    { sku: "lamp", name: "Desk Lamp",   priceCents: 2999, qty: 2 },
    { sku: "arm",  name: "Monitor Arm", priceCents: 5400, qty: 1 },
  ],
  createdAt: new Date("2026-11-01T10:00:00Z"),
});
console.log("--- one order = one read, no joins");
console.log(JSON.stringify(await orders.findOne({ _id: 1 }, { projection: { _id: 1, "customer.name": 1, "items.name": 1, "items.qty": 1 } })));

console.log("--- flexible: documents in one collection can differ");
await orders.insertOne({ _id: 2, customer: { name: "John Smith" }, items: [{ sku: "lamp", qty: 1 }], giftMessage: "Happy birthday!" });
console.log("order 2 has a giftMessage, order 1 doesn't:", (await orders.countDocuments({ giftMessage: { $exists: true } })) === 1);

console.log("--- by default nothing stops bad data");
await orders.insertOne({ _id: 3, custmer: { name: "Typo" }, items: [{ sku: "lamp", qty: -5 }] });   // misspelled field, negative qty
console.log("saved:", JSON.stringify(await orders.findOne({ _id: 3 })));

console.log("--- add rules with a schema validator (after cleaning up the bad document)");
await orders.deleteOne({ _id: 3 });
await db.command({ collMod: "orders", validationLevel: "strict", validator: { $jsonSchema: {
  bsonType: "object", required: ["customer", "items"],
  properties: { items: { bsonType: "array", minItems: 1, items: { bsonType: "object", required: ["sku", "qty"],
    properties: { qty: { bsonType: "int", minimum: 1 } } } } },
} } });
try { await orders.insertOne({ _id: 4, custmer: { name: "Typo" }, items: [{ sku: "lamp", qty: -5 }] }); }
catch (e) { console.log("rejected:", e.code === 121 ? "Document failed validation" : e.message); }

console.log("--- copies must be updated everywhere");
const r = await orders.updateMany({ "items.sku": "lamp" }, { $set: { "items.$[i].name": "LED Desk Lamp" } }, { arrayFilters: [{ "i.sku": "lamp" }] });
console.log(`renamed the lamp inside ${r.modifiedCount} order documents`);

await client.close();
EOF
until docker exec mongo-demo mongosh --quiet --eval 'db.runCommand({ ping: 1 }).ok' > /dev/null 2>&1; do sleep 0.5; done
node docs.js
docker rm -f mongo-demo > /dev/null
```

```text output
--- one order = one read, no joins
{"_id":1,"customer":{"name":"Jane Doe"},"items":[{"name":"Desk Lamp","qty":2},{"name":"Monitor Arm","qty":1}]}
--- flexible: documents in one collection can differ
order 2 has a giftMessage, order 1 doesn't: true
--- by default nothing stops bad data
saved: {"_id":3,"custmer":{"name":"Typo"},"items":[{"sku":"lamp","qty":-5}]}
--- add rules with a schema validator (after cleaning up the bad document)
rejected: Document failed validation
--- copies must be updated everywhere
renamed the lamp inside 2 order documents
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- One `findOne` returned the whole order with its customer and items: **no joins**. If your app almost always reads an order as a whole, this is simpler and fast.
- Documents in one collection can have **different shapes**: order 2 has a `giftMessage` field, order 1 doesn't, and no migration was needed.
- That flexibility cuts both ways: a document with a misspelled field (`custmer`) and a **negative quantity** was saved without complaint. In a relational database, the column wouldn't exist and the `CHECK` would fail.
- A **`$jsonSchema` validator** brought rules back: the same broken document was rejected. (We deleted the bad document first: with `validationLevel: "strict"`, existing invalid documents block later updates to themselves.)
- Renaming the lamp needed an **`updateMany`** across every order that contains a copy. Here that's fine (an order should keep the name it was sold under, a snapshot), but for data that must always be current, copies become a maintenance burden: you must find and update them all.

</En>
<Ur>

- Ek `findOne` ne poora order us ke customer aur items ke saath lautaya: **koi join nahi**. Agar aap ki app taqreeban hamesha order poora parhti hai to ye saada aur tez hai.
- Ek collection ke documents ki **shaklen mukhtalif** ho sakti hain: order 2 me `giftMessage` field hai, order 1 me nahi, aur koi migration nahi chahiye thi.
- Ye lachak dono taraf kaat'ti hai: ghalat likhe field (`custmer`) aur **manfi miqdar** wala document baghair shikayat save ho gaya. Relational database me column mojood na hota aur `CHECK` nakam hota.
- **`$jsonSchema` validator** ne qawaid wapas laye: wahi toota document rad hua. (Humne pehle bura document mitaya: `validationLevel: "strict"` ke saath mojooda ghalat documents apne baad ke updates rok dete hain.)
- Lamp ka naam badalne ke liye har us order par **`updateMany`** chahiye tha jis me copy hai. Yahan ye theek hai (order ko wahi naam rakhna chahiye jis par bika, ek snapshot), magar jo data hamesha taaza hona chahiye us ke liye copies dekh bhaal ka bojh ban jati hain: sab dhoondh kar update karni parti hain.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

## Common mistakes

<Compare>
<Bad title="Choosing MongoDB because 'no schema means faster development'">

```text
// month 1: { name, email }
// month 6: { name, email } | { fullName, mail } | { name, emails: [] } …
// every reader now handles four shapes; bugs hide in the one nobody remembered
```

</Bad>
<Good title="There is always a schema; decide where it's enforced">

```text
-- relational: in the database
CREATE TABLE users (id serial PRIMARY KEY, name text NOT NULL, email text NOT NULL UNIQUE);

// document: in a $jsonSchema validator AND in the app's types, with migrations for old documents
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Compare>
<Bad title="Using a document store like a relational one">

```text
// orders, customers, products, order_items as separate collections,
// then 4 queries per page to "join" them in application code, with no foreign keys or transactions
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Model for how the data is read">

```text
// document store: embed what's read together (order + its items + a snapshot of names/prices),
// reference what's shared and changes (customerId), and keep relational data in a relational DB
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Tr>
<En>

More mistakes to avoid:

- **Picking a database for scale you don't have**: a single PostgreSQL server handles a huge amount of traffic; most apps never need anything else.
- **Embedding data that grows without limit** (all comments of a post inside the post document): documents have size limits (16 MB in MongoDB) and get slow to rewrite.
- **Thinking NoSQL means no transactions or no SQL needed**: MongoDB has multi-document transactions; many NoSQL stores have SQL-like query languages.
- **Ignoring PostgreSQL's JSON support**: `jsonb` columns give you flexible fields next to strict relational ones.
- **Copying data without deciding who updates the copies**: every denormalized field needs a plan (snapshot on purpose, or update with events).

</En>
<Ur>

Bachne wali mazeed ghaltiyan:

- **Aise scale ke liye database chunna jo aap ke paas nahi**: ek PostgreSQL server bohot zyada traffic sambhalta hai; zyada tar apps ko kabhi kuch aur nahi chahiye.
- **Be-hadd barhne wala data embed karna** (post ke andar us ke saare comments): documents ki size ki hadd hai (MongoDB me 16 MB) aur dobara likhna sust ho jata hai.
- **Ye samajhna ke NoSQL ka matlab transactions nahi ya SQL ki zaroorat nahi**: MongoDB me kai documents ki transactions hain; bohot se NoSQL stores me SQL jaisi query zabanen hain.
- **PostgreSQL ki JSON sahulat nazar andaz karna**: `jsonb` columns sakht relational columns ke saath lachakdar fields dete hain.
- **Ye tay kiye baghair data copy karna ke copies kaun update karega**: har denormalized field ko mansooba chahiye (jaan boojh kar snapshot, ya events se update).

</Ur>
</Tr>

<DeepDive title="More about choosing a database">

**ACID vs BASE.** Relational databases provide ACID transactions (atomic, consistent, isolated, durable). Many distributed NoSQL systems favor availability and **eventual consistency** (BASE), though modern ones often offer stronger options.

**CAP theorem.** In a network partition, a distributed database must choose between consistency and availability; it's a design trade-off, not a reason to avoid relational databases.

**Access-pattern design.** NoSQL modeling starts from the queries ("get an order with its items", "get a user's last 20 events"), not from the entities; DynamoDB single-table design takes this to the extreme.

**Polyglot persistence.** Common setups: PostgreSQL as the source of truth, Redis for caching and sessions, a search engine for full text, a warehouse for analytics.

**PostgreSQL's range.** `jsonb` with indexes, full-text search, geospatial data (PostGIS), time series (TimescaleDB): often enough to avoid a second database.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Real world: the move back to Postgres">

An early-stage marketplace stored everything in MongoDB: users, listings, orders, payouts. It was quick to start, and listings with varied attributes fit documents well.

As the business grew, problems collected. Orders embedded copies of seller details that went stale when sellers changed their payout accounts, and a payout went to an old account. A script with a typo wrote `ammount` instead of `amount` to thousands of documents and nothing complained. Finance reports needed data from five collections and took days of custom code.

The team moved orders, payments and payouts to PostgreSQL with foreign keys, `CHECK` constraints and transactions, and kept product listings in MongoDB, where varied attributes and whole-document reads still fit. A nightly job synced listing summaries into PostgreSQL for reporting.

The lesson they wrote down: choose the model per kind of data, and put money where the database enforces the rules.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

- **Injection exists in both worlds**: SQL injection in relational databases, operator injection in MongoDB (`{ "$gt": "" }` sent as a password); always use parameters and validate types.
- **Constraints are a safety net** against buggy or malicious writes; use them for anything that must never be wrong.
- **Least privilege**: give the app a user that can read and write data, not drop tables or change roles.
- **Copies spread sensitive data**: denormalized personal data must be found and erased everywhere for deletion requests.
- **Never expose a database to the internet**: many MongoDB and Redis breaches came from open instances with no password.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="What's the difference between relational and non-relational databases?">
Relational databases store data in tables with a declared schema, relate them with keys, query with SQL and JOINs, and enforce constraints and transactions. Non-relational databases use other models such as documents, key-value, wide-column or graph, often with flexible schemas and built-in horizontal scaling, trading some guarantees for fit or scale.
</QA>

<QA q="When would you choose MongoDB over PostgreSQL?">
When data is naturally a self-contained document that varies in shape and is read and written as a whole, such as content or catalogs with varied attributes, and you don't need many cross-entity joins or strict relational constraints. PostgreSQL is usually the default otherwise, and its jsonb covers many flexible-field needs.
</QA>

<QA q="What is denormalization and what does it cost?">
Storing copies of related data inside a record to avoid joins and speed up reads. The cost is keeping copies consistent: updates must touch every copy, or the copies must be intentionally treated as snapshots.
</QA>

<QA q="Do NoSQL databases support transactions?">
Many do in some form. MongoDB has atomic single-document operations and multi-document ACID transactions; others offer limited or eventually consistent transactions. Relational databases support multi-row, multi-table transactions as a core feature.
</QA>

<QA q="What is a foreign key and why is it useful?">
A column whose values must match a key in another table. The database guarantees referenced rows exist, preventing orphaned records such as orders for nonexistent customers, and can block or cascade deletes.
</QA>

</DeepDive>
