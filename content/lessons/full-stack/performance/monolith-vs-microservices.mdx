<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
A **monolith** is one application, deployed as one unit, usually with one database. **Microservices** split the system into small services, each owning **its own data**, deployed **independently** and talking over the network (HTTP, gRPC or messages). Microservices let large organizations have many teams ship independently, scale hot parts separately and isolate failures, but they turn function calls into **network calls** that can be slow or fail, and they replace one database transaction with **distributed consistency** problems (sagas, eventual consistency, retries, idempotency). They also need much more operational work: deployment pipelines per service, service discovery, tracing, monitoring. A **modular monolith**, one deployable with strict internal module boundaries, gives most of the code-organization benefits without the distributed-systems costs, and can be split later along those boundaries when a real need appears. The usual advice: **start with a modular monolith**; extract a service when a specific part needs independent scaling, deployment or ownership, not because microservices are fashionable.
</En>
<Ur>
**Monolith** ek application hai, ek unit ki tarah deploy, aam taur par ek database ke saath. **Microservices** system ko chhoti services me baant deti hain, har ek **apne data ki malik**, **alag alag** deploy hoti aur network par baat karti hai (HTTP, gRPC ya messages). Microservices bari tanzeemon me kayi teams ko alag alag ship karne, garam hisson ko alag scale karne aur nakamiyan alag karne deti hain, magar ye function calls ko **network calls** me badalti hain jo sust ya nakam ho sakti hain, aur ek database transaction ki jagah **distributed consistency** ke masle lati hain (sagas, eventual consistency, retries, idempotency). Inhe kaafi zyada operational kaam bhi chahiye: har service ki deployment pipelines, service discovery, tracing, monitoring. **Modular monolith**, sakht andaruni module hadon wala ek deployable, code ki tanzeem ke zyadatar faide distributed systems ki qeemat ke baghair deta hai, aur asal zaroorat aane par baad me unhi hadon par toda ja sakta hai. Aam mashwara: **modular monolith se shuru karein**; service tab nikalein jab kisi khaas hisse ko alag scaling, deployment ya malikana haq chahiye, is liye nahi ke microservices fashion me hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

| --- | --- | --- |
| Deploy | one unit | each service independently |
| Data | one database, transactions across modules | database per service, no cross-service transactions |

| Teams | one codebase, shared releases | teams own and release services independently |
| Operations | simple: one pipeline, one log stream | complex: many pipelines, tracing, service mesh |

<Why>
<Tr>
<En>

- Architecture choices are expensive to reverse and shape how fast a team can move for years.
- Many teams adopted microservices too early and spent their time on infrastructure instead of features.
- Others kept a "big ball of mud" monolith with no boundaries and couldn't change anything safely.
- Knowing the trade-offs lets you pick the simplest structure that solves today's problems.

</En>
<Ur>

- Architecture ke faisle palatna mehnga hai aur saalon tak team ki raftaar tay karte hain.

- Doosri teams ne bina hadon wala "big ball of mud" monolith rakha aur kuch bhi mehfooz tareeqe se badal na sakin.
- Faide aur nuqsaan jaanna aap ko aaj ke masle hal karne wala sab se saada dhancha chunne deta hai.

## Picture it

<Tr>
<En>

Think of a **restaurant**.

- A **monolith** is one kitchen where the chef, the grill and the pastry station share one counter and one stock room. If the dessert can't be made, the chef simply doesn't send out the meal: everything is decided in one place (a transaction).
- **Microservices** are separate food trucks in a food court, each with its own fridge and staff, connected by runners carrying notes. They can open and close independently and you can add more trucks for the busy one, but now a runner can get lost (network failure), and if the dessert truck says no after the grill truck has already cooked the steak, someone has to deal with the wasted steak (compensation).
- A **modular monolith** is one kitchen with clearly separated stations and rules ("only order from the pastry station through its window"): simple to run, and easy to move a station into its own truck later if it really needs it.

</En>
<Ur>

**Restaurant** socheyn.

- **Monolith** ek kitchen hai jahan chef, grill aur pastry station ek counter aur ek godam share karte hain. Agar dessert nahi ban sakta to chef khana bhejta hi nahi: sab kuch ek jagah tay hota hai (transaction).
- **Microservices** food court me alag food trucks hain, har ek ka apna fridge aur staff, notes le jane wale runners se jure. Ye alag alag khul aur band ho sakte hain aur masroof truck ke liye aur truck jor sakte hain, magar ab runner kho sakta hai (network failure), aur agar grill truck ke steak paka lene ke baad dessert truck inkaar kare, to kisi ko zaya steak ka hal nikalna parta hai (compensation).
- **Modular monolith** saaf alag stations aur usoolon wala ek kitchen hai ("pastry station se sirf us ki khirki se order karo"): chalana saada, aur waqai zaroorat ho to baad me kisi station ko apne truck me le jana aasan.

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Words you'll see

<Tr>
<En>

| Word | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Monolith** | One deployable application | a single Express or Nest app |
| **Modular monolith** | Monolith with enforced module boundaries | `orders/`, `inventory/` with public APIs |
| **Microservice** | Small, independently deployable service owning its data | `inventory-service` |
| **Bounded context** | A part of the domain with its own model and language | orders vs billing |
| **Database per service** | Each service's data is private to it | no shared tables |
| **Distributed transaction** | A change spanning several services | order + stock + payment |
| **Saga** | A sequence of local steps with compensating actions | reserve → charge → (release) |
| **Compensation** | An action that undoes an earlier step | release reserved stock |
| **Eventual consistency** | Data agrees across services after a short delay | stock count updates via events |
| **Service discovery** | Finding where a service is running | DNS, Consul, Kubernetes services |
| **Circuit breaker** | Stop calling a failing service for a while | fail fast instead of piling up timeouts |
| **Distributed monolith** | Services so coupled they must deploy together | the worst of both |

</En>
<Ur>

| Lafz | Matlab | Misaal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Monolith** | Ek deployable application | ek Express ya Nest app |
| **Modular monolith** | Lagu shuda module hadon wala monolith | public APIs ke saath `orders/`, `inventory/` |
| **Microservice** | Apne data ki malik, alag deploy hone wali chhoti service | `inventory-service` |
| **Bounded context** | Domain ka hissa jis ka apna model aur zaban ho | orders aur billing |
| **Database per service** | Har service ka data us ka nijji | shared tables nahi |

| **Service discovery** | Service kahan chal rahi hai dhoondna | DNS, Consul, Kubernetes services |
| **Circuit breaker** | Nakam service ko kuch dair bulana band | timeouts jama karne ke bajaye jaldi nakam |
| **Distributed monolith** | Itni juri services ke saath deploy karna pare | dono ki burai |

</Ur>

## Learn it step by step

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="The order flow in a modular monolith">

<Tr>
<En>
One Node process with three modules: `inventory` reserves stock, `payments` charges (and declines anything over $100), `orders` coordinates. Stock and orders live in one PostgreSQL database, so placing an order is **one transaction**. We buy 2 lamps at $35, then 3 at $50 (declined), print stock and order counts after each, and time 10,000 module calls.
</En>
<Ur>
Teen modules wala ek Node process: `inventory` stock reserve karta hai, `payments` charge karta hai (aur $100 se zyada sab inkaar), `orders` tanzeem karta hai. Stock aur orders ek PostgreSQL database me hain, isliye order dena **ek transaction** hai. Hum $35 par 2 lamps khareedte hain, phir $50 par 3 (inkaar), har ek ke baad stock aur orders ki ginti chhapte hain, aur 10,000 module calls ka waqt napte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
npm init -y > /dev/null && npm pkg set type=module && npm install pg@8.23.1 --silent
export PGPASSWORD=postgres
psql -q -h localhost -U postgres -c 'DROP DATABASE IF EXISTS shop_mono' -c 'CREATE DATABASE shop_mono' 2> /dev/null
psql -q -h localhost -U postgres -d shop_mono -c "CREATE TABLE stock (sku text PRIMARY KEY, qty int CHECK (qty >= 0))" \
  -c "CREATE TABLE orders (id serial PRIMARY KEY, sku text, qty int, status text)" -c "INSERT INTO stock VALUES ('lamp', 5)"
cat > monolith.js <<'EOF'
// A MODULAR MONOLITH: one deployable, clear modules, one database
import pg from 'pg';
const db = new pg.Pool({ connectionString: 'postgres://postgres:postgres@localhost:5432/shop_mono' });

const inventory = { reserve: (tx, sku, qty) => tx.query('UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - $1 WHERE sku = $2', [qty, sku]) };
const payments = { charge: async (amountCents) => { if (amountCents > 10000) throw new Error('card declined'); } };
const orders = {
  async place(sku, qty, priceCents) {
    const tx = await db.connect();
    try {
      await tx.query('BEGIN');
      await inventory.reserve(tx, sku, qty);                       // module call = a function call
      const { rows: [o] } = await tx.query("INSERT INTO orders (sku, qty, status) VALUES ($1, $2, 'paid') RETURNING id", [sku, qty]);
      await payments.charge(qty * priceCents);
      await tx.query('COMMIT');                                    // all or nothing
      return `order ${o.id} placed`;
    } catch (e) { await tx.query('ROLLBACK'); return `failed (${e.message}), everything rolled back`; }
    finally { tx.release(); }
  },
};
const state = async () => { const s = await db.query("SELECT qty FROM stock WHERE sku = 'lamp'"); const o = await db.query('SELECT count(*)::int AS n FROM orders'); return `stock ${s.rows[0].qty}, orders ${o.rows[0].n}`; };

console.log('buy 2 lamps ($35 each):  ', await orders.place('lamp', 2, 3500), '|', await state());
console.log('buy 3 lamps ($50 each):  ', await orders.place('lamp', 3, 5000), '|', await state());

// How expensive is a module call? Time 10,000 of them
let t = performance.now();
for (let i = 0; i < 10000; i++) await payments.charge(100);
console.log(`10,000 in-process calls: ${(performance.now() - t) < 100 ? 'under 100 ms total' : 'slow?'}`);
await db.end();
EOF
node monolith.js
```

```text output
buy 2 lamps ($35 each):   order 1 placed | stock 3, orders 1
buy 3 lamps ($50 each):   failed (card declined), everything rolled back | stock 3, orders 1
10,000 in-process calls: under 100 ms total
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- The first order reserved 2 lamps and saved the order: **stock 3, orders 1**.
- The second order reserved 3 lamps and inserted an order row, then the payment was **declined**. `ROLLBACK` undid **both** the stock change and the order row, so the data stayed exactly as before: no missing lamps, no half-made order. You didn't write any undo code; the database did it.
- Modules still have clear boundaries (`inventory.reserve`, `payments.charge`), so the code is organized like services would be, but calls between them are plain function calls: 10,000 of them took well under 100 ms in total, and they can't time out halfway.

</En>
<Ur>

- Pehle order ne 2 lamps reserve kiye aur order save kiya: **stock 3, orders 1**.
- Doosre order ne 3 lamps reserve kiye aur order row daali, phir payment **inkaar** hui. `ROLLBACK` ne stock ki tabdeeli aur order row **dono** wapas kin, isliye data bilkul pehle jaisa raha: koi lamps ghayab nahi, koi adhura order nahi. Aap ne undo ka koi code nahi likha; database ne kiya.
- Modules ki hadein ab bhi saaf hain (`inventory.reserve`, `payments.charge`), isliye code services ki tarah tarteeb shuda hai, magar un ke beech calls saade function calls hain: 10,000 calls ne kul 100 ms se kaafi kam waqt liya, aur beech me time out nahi ho saktin.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="The same flow as three microservices">

<Tr>
<En>
`services.js` runs the same logic as **three processes**: `inventory` (port 4001) and `payments` (4002) each own their data, and `orders` (4000) calls them over HTTP. We repeat the declined order **without** and **with** a saga compensation (release the reserved stock), stop the inventory service, and time 1,000 HTTP calls to compare with the function calls above.
</En>
<Ur>
`services.js` wahi logic **teen processes** ki tarah chalata hai: `inventory` (port 4001) aur `payments` (4002) apna apna data rakhte hain, aur `orders` (4000) unhe HTTP par bulata hai. Hum inkaar hone wala order compensation ke **baghair** aur saga compensation **ke saath** (reserve stock wapas) dohrate hain, inventory service band karte hain, aur oopar ke function calls se muqable ke liye 1,000 HTTP calls ka waqt napte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
cat > services.js <<'EOF'
// The same shop as MICROSERVICES: three processes, each with its own data, talking over HTTP
import http from 'node:http';
const json = (res, status, body) => { res.writeHead(status, { 'content-type': 'application/json' }); res.end(JSON.stringify(body)); };
const read = async (req) => { let b = ''; for await (const c of req) b += c; return b ? JSON.parse(b) : {}; };
const service = (port, routes) => http.createServer(async (req, res) => {
  const handler = routes[`${req.method} ${req.url}`];
  handler ? handler(await read(req), res) : json(res, 404, {});
}).listen(port);

if (process.argv[2] === 'inventory') {
  let stock = 5;                                                              // inventory owns its data
  service(4001, {
    'POST /reserve': ({ qty }, res) => (stock >= qty ? (stock -= qty, json(res, 200, { stock })) : json(res, 409, { error: 'out of stock' })),
    'POST /release': ({ qty }, res) => { stock += qty; json(res, 200, { stock }); },
    'GET /stock': (_, res) => json(res, 200, { stock }),
  });
}
if (process.argv[2] === 'payments')
  service(4002, { 'POST /charge': ({ amountCents }, res) => (amountCents > 10000 ? json(res, 402, { error: 'card declined' }) : json(res, 200, { ok: true })) });

if (process.argv[2] === 'orders') {
  const orders = [];
  const call = async (url, body) => {
    try { const r = await fetch(url, { method: 'POST', headers: { 'content-type': 'application/json' }, body: JSON.stringify(body), signal: AbortSignal.timeout(1000) }); return { ok: r.ok, ...(await r.json()) }; }
    catch { return { ok: false, error: 'service unavailable' }; }
  };
  service(4000, {
    'POST /orders': async ({ qty, priceCents, compensate }, res) => {
      const reserved = await call('http://localhost:4001/reserve', { qty });       // network hop 1
      if (!reserved.ok) return json(res, 503, { error: `inventory: ${reserved.error}` });
      const paid = await call('http://localhost:4002/charge', { amountCents: qty * priceCents });   // network hop 2
      if (!paid.ok) {
        if (compensate) await call('http://localhost:4001/release', { qty });  // SAGA: undo step 1 by hand
        return json(res, 402, { error: paid.error });
      }
      orders.push({ qty }); json(res, 201, { order: orders.length });
    },
    'GET /count': (_, res) => json(res, 200, { orders: orders.length }),
  });
}
EOF
node services.js inventory & I=$!; node services.js payments & P=$!; node services.js orders & O=$!; sleep 1
order() { curl -s -X POST -H 'content-type: application/json' -d "$1" localhost:4000/orders; }
state() { echo "stock $(curl -s localhost:4001/stock | grep -o '[0-9]*'), orders $(curl -s localhost:4000/count | grep -o '[0-9]*')"; }
echo "buy 2 lamps (\$35):                  $(order '{"qty":2,"priceCents":3500}') | $(state)"
echo "buy 3 lamps (\$50), no compensation: $(order '{"qty":3,"priceCents":5000}') | $(state)   <- 3 lamps vanished"
curl -s -X POST -H 'content-type: application/json' -d '{"qty":3}' localhost:4001/release > /dev/null    # (fix the data by hand)
echo "buy 3 lamps (\$50), with a saga:     $(order '{"qty":3,"priceCents":5000,"compensate":true}') | $(state)"
kill $I; wait $I 2> /dev/null
echo "inventory service is down:          $(order '{"qty":1,"priceCents":3500}')"
node -e "
let t = performance.now();
for (let i = 0; i < 1000; i++) await fetch('http://localhost:4002/charge', { method: 'POST', body: '{\"amountCents\":100}' });
const ms = performance.now() - t;
console.log('1,000 HTTP calls on the same machine:', ms > 100 ? 'over 100 ms total (each hop costs far more than a function call)' : 'fast?');
" --input-type=module
kill $P $O; wait $P $O 2> /dev/null || true
```

```text output
buy 2 lamps ($35):                  {"order":1} | stock 3, orders 1
buy 3 lamps ($50), no compensation: {"error":"card declined"} | stock 0, orders 1   <- 3 lamps vanished
buy 3 lamps ($50), with a saga:     {"error":"card declined"} | stock 3, orders 1
inventory service is down:          {"error":"inventory: service unavailable"}
1,000 HTTP calls on the same machine: over 100 ms total (each hop costs far more than a function call)
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- The happy path works the same: 2 lamps reserved, order created.
- The declined order **without compensation** left **stock 0, orders 1**: inventory had already committed its reservation in its own process and data, and there's no shared transaction to roll back. Three lamps "vanished" until we fixed the data by hand.
- **With a saga**, the orders service called `/release` after the payment failed, and stock went back to 3. Compensations are code you must write, test and make reliable: what if the release call itself fails? Real systems persist saga state, retry with idempotency keys, or use a message broker and an outbox (next lesson).
- When inventory was **down**, checkout failed with "service unavailable" after a timeout. In a monolith, that module can't be down while the app is up. Microservices need timeouts, retries, circuit breakers and graceful degradation everywhere.
- 1,000 HTTP calls on the same machine took **over 100 ms**, far more than 10,000 function calls; across real networks each hop costs milliseconds more, plus serialization, auth and logging. Chatty services get slow quickly.

</En>
<Ur>

- Seedha rasta waisa hi chalta hai: 2 lamps reserve, order bana.
- **Compensation ke baghair** inkaar wale order ne **stock 0, orders 1** chhora: inventory apne process aur data me reservation pehle hi commit kar chuki thi, aur roll back ke liye koi shared transaction nahi. Teen lamps tab tak "ghayab" rahe jab tak hum ne haath se data theek nahi kiya.
- **Saga ke saath**, payment nakam hone ke baad orders service ne `/release` bulaya, aur stock wapas 3 hua. Compensations aisa code hain jo aap ko likhna, test karna aur qabil-e-bharosa banana hai: agar release call khud nakam ho to? Asli systems saga ki halat mehfooz rakhte, idempotency keys ke saath retry karte, ya message broker aur outbox istemal karte hain (agla lesson).
- Jab inventory **band** thi, checkout timeout ke baad "service unavailable" ke saath nakam hua. Monolith me ye module band nahi ho sakta jab app chal rahi ho. Microservices ko har jagah timeouts, retries, circuit breakers aur behtar tareeqe se kam hona chahiye.
- Ek hi machine par 1,000 HTTP calls ne **100 ms se zyada** liye, 10,000 function calls se kaafi zyada; asli networks par har hop milliseconds aur leta hai, plus serialization, auth aur logging. Bohot baat karne wali services jaldi sust ho jati hain.

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Common mistakes

<Compare>
<Bad title="Microservices sharing one database">

```text
orders-service    ──┐
inventory-service ──┼──> one Postgres with shared tables
payments-service  ──┘
// every schema change needs all teams; services must deploy together: a distributed monolith
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Each service owns its data, others use its API or events">

```text
inventory-service -> inventory DB      (others call POST /reserve or listen to stock.changed)
orders-service    -> orders DB
// or stay a modular monolith with module-owned tables until a split is truly needed
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Compare>
<Bad title="Splitting by technical layer">

```text
users-api, users-db-service, users-validation-service, email-service-for-users
// one feature change touches four services and four deploys
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Splitting by business capability">

```text
catalog, ordering, billing, shipping
// a feature usually lives inside one service owned by one team
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Tr>
<En>

More mistakes to avoid:

- **Starting with microservices** for a new product before the domain is understood: boundaries drawn too early are expensive to move.
- **Synchronous call chains** (A → B → C → D): latency adds up and any failure breaks the whole request.
- **No timeouts or retries with backoff**, or retries without idempotency, causing duplicate charges.
- **Missing observability**: without distributed tracing and correlated logs, debugging across services is guesswork.
- **A monolith without boundaries**: modules reaching into each other's tables make any later split nearly impossible.
- **Splitting for performance** without measuring: a monolith scaled horizontally handles a lot.

</En>
<Ur>

Bachne wali mazeed ghaltiyan:

- Domain samajhne se pehle naye product ke liye **microservices se shuru karna**: jaldi khinchi hadein badalna mehnga hai.
- **Synchronous call chains** (A → B → C → D): dair jurti jati hai aur koi bhi nakami poori request torti hai.
- **Backoff ke saath timeouts ya retries nahi**, ya idempotency ke baghair retries, jo do dafa charge karati hain.
- **Observability ghayab**: distributed tracing aur jure logs ke baghair services ke paar debug karna andaza hai.
- **Bina hadon wala monolith**: ek doosre ke tables me haath dalne wale modules baad me todna taqreeban na-mumkin banate hain.
- **Bina pemaish performance ke liye todna**: horizontally scale kiya monolith bohot kuch sambhal leta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>

<Challenge title="Make the saga reliable">

<Tr>
<En>
In the demo, what happens if the `/release` call fails after a declined payment? Design a more reliable version of the order saga.
</En>
<Ur>
Demo me, agar inkaar shuda payment ke baad `/release` call nakam ho to kya hota hai? Order saga ka zyada qabil-e-bharosa version design karein.
</Ur>
</Tr>

<Solution>

<Tr>
<En>

If `/release` fails, the stock stays reserved forever: the same "vanished lamps" bug, just rarer. A more reliable design:

1. Orders stores the saga in its own database: `order(id, status: 'reserving' | 'charging' | 'compensating' | 'done' | 'failed')`, updated after each step.
2. Every call carries an **idempotency key** (the order id), so retrying `reserve`, `charge` or `release` never applies twice.
3. A background worker retries unfinished sagas with backoff until they reach `done` or `failed`; failed compensations alert a human.
4. Reservations get an **expiry** in the inventory service, so even a lost release eventually frees stock.
5. Optionally, switch to messages (`order.created` → inventory reserves → `stock.reserved` → payments charges…) with an outbox, so steps survive restarts.

</En>
<Ur>

Agar `/release` nakam ho to stock hamesha reserve rehta hai: wahi "ghayab lamps" wala bug, bas kam hota. Zyada qabil-e-bharosa design:

1. Orders saga ko apne database me rakhta hai: `order(id, status: 'reserving' | 'charging' | 'compensating' | 'done' | 'failed')`, har qadam ke baad update.
2. Har call **idempotency key** (order id) le jati hai, isliye `reserve`, `charge` ya `release` ka retry kabhi do dafa nahi lagta.
3. Background worker adhoore sagas ko backoff ke saath `done` ya `failed` tak retry karta hai; nakam compensations insaan ko alert karti hain.
4. Inventory service me reservations ki **expiry** hoti hai, isliye khoya release bhi aakhir stock azad kar deta hai.
5. Chahein to messages par jayen (`order.created` → inventory reserve → `stock.reserved` → payments charge…) outbox ke saath, taa ke qadam restarts ke baad bhi bache rahen.

<DeepDive title="More about architecture choices">

**Conway's law.** Systems mirror the communication structure of the organizations that build them; service boundaries work best when they match team boundaries.

**Domain-driven design.** Bounded contexts and context maps help find natural seams for modules or services.

**Strangler fig pattern.** Migrate a legacy monolith gradually: route one capability at a time to a new service behind a proxy until the old code can be removed.

**Enforcing module boundaries.** Lint rules (eslint-plugin-boundaries, dependency-cruiser), Nx module boundaries, or separate packages in a monorepo keep a modular monolith modular.

**Communication styles.** Synchronous (REST, gRPC) for queries; asynchronous messages for workflows and decoupling; avoid long synchronous chains.

**Operational building blocks.** API gateways, service meshes (Istio, Linkerd), distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry), centralized logging and per-service SLOs.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Real world: back to a monolith">

A video-monitoring team inside a large company had built their quality-checking system as distributed components, each a separate serverless function or service passing frames and results through storage and orchestration steps. As usage grew, the cost of moving data between components and the orchestration overhead dominated; the system hit scaling limits and costs climbed. The team consolidated the components into a single process that passed data in memory, running it on regular instances, and reported a large cost reduction and better scalability.

The lessons teams took from it:

1. Distribution has a price (network, serialization, orchestration) that is easy to underestimate on paper.
2. Architecture isn't permanent; measuring real costs and moving in either direction is a sign of maturity.
3. Splitting along the wrong boundary (where data flows constantly) is worse than not splitting.
4. "Microservices vs monolith" matters less than clear boundaries, good data flow and observability.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

- **More services, more attack surface**: every service-to-service call needs authentication (mTLS, signed tokens) and authorization, not "trusted internal network".
- **Secrets multiply**: each service has credentials; use a secret manager and least privilege per service.
- **Data ownership helps**: isolating sensitive data (payments, PII) in one service limits who can touch it.
- **Monoliths need internal boundaries too**: one SQL injection in a module can read every table, so use database roles and schemas where it matters.
- **Consistent security controls**: a shared library or gateway for auth, rate limiting and logging avoids each service reinventing (and forgetting) them.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="What are the pros and cons of microservices?">
Pros: independent deployment and scaling, team autonomy, fault isolation and technology flexibility. Cons: network latency and failures, distributed data consistency, more complex testing, debugging and operations, and higher infrastructure cost.
</QA>

<QA q="When would you choose a monolith?">
For new products, small teams, changing requirements, strong consistency needs or limited operations capacity, ideally as a modular monolith with clear boundaries that can be split later.
</QA>

<QA q="How do you handle a transaction across microservices?">
Avoid it if possible by drawing boundaries around consistency needs. Otherwise use a saga of local transactions with compensating actions, coordinated by orchestration or choreography with messages, using idempotency keys, retries and an outbox for reliability.
</QA>

<QA q="What is a distributed monolith and how do you avoid it?">
Services that are so tightly coupled, through shared databases, synchronous chains or lockstep releases, that they can't be deployed independently. Avoid it with data ownership per service, asynchronous communication and backward-compatible APIs.
</QA>

<QA q="How would you split a monolith?">
First modularize it internally and enforce boundaries, identify a capability with clear ownership and a real reason to separate, move its data behind its own API, route traffic to the new service gradually with the strangler fig pattern, and add observability before and during the move.
</QA>

</DeepDive>
