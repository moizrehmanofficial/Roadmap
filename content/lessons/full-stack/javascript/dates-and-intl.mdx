<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
A JavaScript **`Date`** is surprisingly simple inside: it's just **one number**, the milliseconds since 1 January 1970 at midnight **UTC** (the "epoch"). There's no time zone stored in it. What makes dates hard is everything **around** that number: people read times in **their own time zone**, months have different lengths, clocks jump for **daylight saving**, and every country writes dates differently (11/12/2026 or 12/11/2026?). The safe approach is a **golden rule**: create and **store** moments in **UTC** (ISO strings like `2026-11-12T14:30:00Z`, or timestamps), do math in milliseconds or with UTC methods, and only at the very end **display** them with **`Intl.DateTimeFormat`** in the reader's locale and time zone. The `Intl` family also handles relative times ("3 days ago"), lists ("A, B, and C") and plural rules.
</En>
<Ur>
JavaScript ka **`Date`** andar se hairat angez had tak saada hai: ye bas **ek number** hai, 1 January 1970 ki aadhi raat **UTC** (the "epoch") se milliseconds. Is me koi time zone mehfooz nahi hota. Dates ko mushkil wo sab banata hai jo us number ke **ird gird** hai: log waqt **apne time zone** me parhte hain, mahinon ki lambai mukhtalif hai, **daylight saving** ke liye ghariyan uchhalti hain, aur har mulk dates alag likhta hai (11/12/2026 ya 12/11/2026?). Mehfooz tareeqa ek **sunehra qaida** hai: lamhe **UTC** me banayen aur **mehfooz** karein (ISO strings jaise `2026-11-12T14:30:00Z`, ya timestamps), hisaab milliseconds ya UTC methods se karein, aur bilkul aakhir me parhne wale ki locale aur time zone me **`Intl.DateTimeFormat`** se **dikhayen**. `Intl` khandan relative waqt ("3 days ago"), lists ("A, B, and C") aur plural ke qawaid bhi sambhalta hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>
</BigIdea>

<Why>
<Tr>
<En>

- **Almost every app shows dates**: order times, deadlines, "last seen", schedules, invoices.
- **Date bugs are expensive**: a deadline shown a day early, a subscription renewed twice, a meeting at the wrong hour for half the team.
- **Users live in different time zones and write dates differently.** Showing `11/12/2026` to a British user means 11 December to them.
- **`Intl` is built in**: no library needed for most formatting.

</En>
<Ur>

- **Taqreeban har app dates dikhati hai**: order ka waqt, deadlines, "last seen", schedules, invoices.
- **Date ke bugs mehnge hote hain**: ek din pehle dikhai deadline, do dafa renew hui subscription, aadhi team ke liye ghalat ghante par meeting.
- **Users mukhtalif time zones me rehte hain aur dates alag likhte hain.** British user ko `11/12/2026` dikhana un ke liye 11 December hai.
- **`Intl` bana banaya hai**: zyada tar formatting ke liye library ki zaroorat nahi.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Why>

## Picture it

<Tr>
<En>

Think of a **single master clock** in a laboratory that counts milliseconds since 1970 and never changes for seasons or countries. That count is what a `Date` holds.

- Every **office around the world** has a screen showing that same moment translated into local time: 2:30 PM in London, 9:30 AM in New York, 11:30 PM in Tokyo. The **moment** is one; the **displays** differ.
- **Daylight saving** is an office deciding to shift its screen by an hour twice a year. The master clock doesn't care; only the display jumps.
- Writing dates on paper is like **each office using its own handwriting**: Americans write month first, the British day first, Germans use dots. `Intl.DateTimeFormat` is a translator who knows every office's handwriting.
- The golden rule: **record by the master clock, translate for the reader.**

</En>
<Ur>

Laboratory me ek **master clock** sochein jo 1970 se milliseconds ginti hai aur mausam ya mulkon ke liye kabhi nahi badalti. Wahi ginti `Date` me hoti hai.

- **Dunya bhar ke har office** me ek screen hai jo wahi lamha local waqt me tarjuma kar ke dikhati hai: London me 2:30 PM, New York me 9:30 AM, Tokyo me 11:30 PM. **Lamha** ek hai; **dikhawe** mukhtalif.
- **Daylight saving** kisi office ka saal me do dafa apni screen ek ghante khiskane ka faisla hai. Master clock ko parwah nahi; sirf dikhawa uchhalta hai.
- Kaghaz par dates likhna **har office ki apni likhai** jaisa hai: Americans pehle mahina likhte hain, British pehle din, Germans dots lagate hain. `Intl.DateTimeFormat` aisa tarjuman hai jo har office ki likhai jaanta hai.
- Sunehra qaida: **master clock se darj karo, parhne wale ke liye tarjuma karo.**

</Ur>
</Tr>

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="A Date is a timestamp">

<Tr>
<En>
A `Date` stores one number: milliseconds since the epoch, in UTC. You can create it from an ISO string, from parts with `Date.UTC`, or from a number. `toISOString()` gives the standard UTC text, and that's also what JSON uses.
</En>
<Ur>
`Date` ek number mehfooz karta hai: epoch se milliseconds, UTC me. Aap ise ISO string se, `Date.UTC` ke hisson se, ya number se bana sakte hain. `toISOString()` mayari UTC text deta hai, aur JSON bhi yahi istemal karta hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const launch = new Date("2026-11-12T14:30:00Z");
console.log(launch.toISOString());
console.log(launch.getTime(), typeof launch.getTime());

const fromParts = new Date(Date.UTC(2026, 10, 12, 14, 30));
console.log(fromParts.toISOString(), fromParts.getTime() === launch.getTime());

console.log(new Date(0).toISOString());
console.log(JSON.stringify({ launch }));
```

```text output
2026-11-12T14:30:00.000Z
1794493800000 number
2026-11-12T14:30:00.000Z true
1970-01-01T00:00:00.000Z
{"launch":"2026-11-12T14:30:00.000Z"}
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `getTime()` revealed the single number inside: 1,794,493,800,000 ms after the epoch.
- `Date.UTC(2026, 10, 12, …)` built the same moment from parts. Note the **10** for November (next step explains).
- `new Date(0)` is the epoch itself.
- `JSON.stringify` turned the date into its ISO string. When you read it back, it's a **string** again (JSON lesson: use a reviver).
- `Date.now()` gives the current timestamp; we don't print it here because it changes every millisecond.

</En>
<Ur>

- `getTime()` ne andar ka wahid number dikhaya: epoch ke baad 1,794,493,800,000 ms.
- `Date.UTC(2026, 10, 12, …)` ne hisson se wahi lamha banaya. November ke liye **10** par ghaur karein (agla qadam samjhata hai).
- `new Date(0)` khud epoch hai.
- `JSON.stringify` ne date ko us ki ISO string me badla. Wapas parhne par ye dobara **string** hoti hai (JSON lesson: reviver lein).
- `Date.now()` maujooda timestamp deta hai; hum yahan use print nahi karte kyun ke ye har millisecond badalta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Reading parts, and the zero-based month">

<Tr>
<En>
UTC getters read parts of the date: `getUTCFullYear`, `getUTCMonth`, `getUTCDate` (day of month), `getUTCDay` (day of week), `getUTCHours`. **Months start at 0** (January = 0, December = 11), and days of the week start at Sunday = 0. Out-of-range values roll over.
</En>
<Ur>
UTC getters date ke hisse parhte hain: `getUTCFullYear`, `getUTCMonth`, `getUTCDate` (mahine ka din), `getUTCDay` (hafte ka din), `getUTCHours`. **Mahine 0 se shuru hote hain** (January = 0, December = 11), aur hafte ke din Sunday = 0 se. Hadd se bahar values aage lurhak jati hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const d = new Date("2026-11-12T14:30:00Z");
console.log(d.getUTCFullYear(), d.getUTCMonth(), d.getUTCDate(), d.getUTCDay(), d.getUTCHours());

const months = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun", "Jul", "Aug", "Sep", "Oct", "Nov", "Dec"];
console.log(months[d.getUTCMonth()]);

const surprise = new Date(Date.UTC(2026, 12, 1));
console.log(surprise.toISOString());
```

```text output
2026 10 12 4 14
Nov
2027-01-01T00:00:00.000Z
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- November printed as **10**. This is the most famous date bug: always remember `+ 1` when showing a month number, or use `Intl` to format it.
- `getUTCDay()` is 4: Thursday (Sunday is 0).
- Month **12** doesn't exist, so `Date.UTC(2026, 12, 1)` silently rolled over to **January 2027**. JavaScript doesn't complain about out-of-range parts; it adds them up. (This is handy for date math, dangerous for typos.)

</En>
<Ur>

- November **10** ki shakal me print hua. Ye sab se mashhoor date bug hai: mahine ka number dikhate waqt hamesha `+ 1` yaad rakhein, ya `Intl` se format karein.
- `getUTCDay()` 4 hai: Thursday (Sunday 0 hai).
- Mahina **12** maujood nahi, isliye `Date.UTC(2026, 12, 1)` khamoshi se **January 2027** par lurhak gaya. JavaScript hadd se bahar hisson par shikayat nahi karti; unhe jor deti hai. (Date ke hisaab ke liye kaam ka, typos ke liye khatarnak.)

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Parsing strings, and Invalid Date">

<Tr>
<En>
Only **ISO 8601** strings are parsed reliably everywhere. A date-only string (`"2026-11-12"`) is treated as **UTC midnight**, and a string with an explicit `Z` or offset is exact. Anything else (`"31/12/2026"`, `"next tuesday"`) gives an **Invalid Date**, whose time is `NaN`.
</En>
<Ur>
Sirf **ISO 8601** strings har jagah qabil-e-bharosa tareeqe se parse hoti hain. Sirf date wali string (`"2026-11-12"`) **UTC aadhi raat** maani jati hai, aur wazeh `Z` ya offset wali string bilkul durust hoti hai. Baqi sab (`"31/12/2026"`, `"next tuesday"`) **Invalid Date** deta hai, jis ka waqt `NaN` hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const tests = ["2026-11-12", "2026-11-12T10:00:00Z", "2026-11-12T10:00:00+05:00", "31/12/2026", "next tuesday"];

for (const t of tests) {
  const d = new Date(t);
  console.log(t.padEnd(26), Number.isNaN(d.getTime()) ? "Invalid Date" : d.toISOString());
}
```

```text output
2026-11-12                 2026-11-12T00:00:00.000Z
2026-11-12T10:00:00Z       2026-11-12T10:00:00.000Z
2026-11-12T10:00:00+05:00  2026-11-12T05:00:00.000Z
31/12/2026                 Invalid Date
next tuesday               Invalid Date
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `"2026-11-12"` became midnight **UTC**. In New York that's still the evening of 11 November, so showing it in local time can display the **previous day**. Treat date-only values as text, not moments.
- The `+05:00` offset was converted: 10:00 at +5 hours = 05:00 UTC.
- Watch out for one rule the table doesn't show: a date-**time** string **without** `Z` or an offset (`"2026-11-12T10:00"`) is read in the **computer's local time zone**, so the same code gives different moments on different machines. Always include `Z` or an offset.
- Invalid dates don't throw. Check with `Number.isNaN(date.getTime())` before using them; otherwise `toISOString()` throws a `RangeError` later.

</En>
<Ur>

- `"2026-11-12"` **UTC** ki aadhi raat bani. New York me ye ab bhi 11 November ki shaam hai, isliye ise local waqt me dikhana **pichhla din** dikha sakta hai. Sirf date wali values ko lamhe nahi, text samjhein.
- `+05:00` offset badla gaya: +5 ghante par 10:00 = 05:00 UTC.
- Ek qaide se ehtiyat karein jo table nahi dikhati: **`Z` ya offset ke baghair** date-**time** string (`"2026-11-12T10:00"`) **computer ke local time zone** me parhi jati hai, isliye wahi code mukhtalif machines par mukhtalif lamhe deta hai. Hamesha `Z` ya offset shamil karein.
- Invalid dates error nahi phenktin. Istemal se pehle `Number.isNaN(date.getTime())` se check karein; warna `toISOString()` baad me `RangeError` phenkta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Date math">

<Tr>
<En>
Subtracting two dates gives **milliseconds**; divide to get days or hours. To add calendar days or months, **copy** the date, then use `setUTCDate` or `setUTCMonth` (setters **mutate**). Compare dates with `<`/`>` or `getTime()`, never `===`.
</En>
<Ur>
Do dates ghatane se **milliseconds** milte hain; din ya ghante lene ke liye taqseem karein. Calendar ke din ya mahine jorne ke liye date ki **copy** banayen, phir `setUTCDate` ya `setUTCMonth` lein (setters **mutate** karte hain). Dates ka muqabla `<`/`>` ya `getTime()` se karein, kabhi `===` se nahi.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const DAY = 24 * 60 * 60 * 1000;
const ordered = new Date("2026-11-12T09:00:00Z");
const delivered = new Date("2026-11-15T17:00:00Z");

const ms = delivered - ordered;
console.log(ms, (ms / DAY).toFixed(2), "days", Math.floor(ms / DAY), "full days");

function addDays(date, n) {
  const copy = new Date(date);
  copy.setUTCDate(copy.getUTCDate() + n);
  return copy;
}
console.log(addDays(ordered, 30).toISOString(), ordered.toISOString());

const jan31 = new Date("2026-01-31T00:00:00Z");
const plusMonth = new Date(jan31);
plusMonth.setUTCMonth(plusMonth.getUTCMonth() + 1);
console.log(plusMonth.toISOString());

const a = new Date("2026-11-12T09:00:00Z");
const b = new Date("2026-11-12T09:00:00Z");
console.log(a === b, a.getTime() === b.getTime(), a < delivered);
```

```text output
288000000 3.33 days 3 full days
2026-12-12T09:00:00.000Z 2026-11-12T09:00:00.000Z
2026-03-03T00:00:00.000Z
false true true
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `delivered - ordered` converted both dates to numbers automatically: 288,000,000 ms ≈ 3.33 days.
- `addDays` copied the date first, so `ordered` was unchanged. Adding 30 days rolled correctly into December.
- **January 31 + 1 month = March 3**: February 31 doesn't exist, so it overflowed into March. Libraries usually clamp to the last day of the month (Feb 28) instead. Decide which rule your business needs.
- Two `Date` objects for the same moment aren't `===` (they're different objects); compare `getTime()`.

</En>
<Ur>

- `delivered - ordered` ne dono dates ko khud numbers me badla: 288,000,000 ms ≈ 3.33 din.
- `addDays` ne pehle date copy ki, isliye `ordered` nahi badla. 30 din jorna sahi tarah December me lurhak gaya.
- **January 31 + 1 mahina = March 3**: February 31 maujood nahi, isliye ye March me chhalak gaya. Libraries aam taur par is ke bajaye mahine ke aakhri din (Feb 28) par rok deti hain. Tay karein ke aap ke business ko kaun sa qaida chahiye.
- Ek hi lamhe ke do `Date` objects `===` nahi (ye alag objects hain); `getTime()` compare karein.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Formatting with Intl.DateTimeFormat">

<Tr>
<En>
`Intl.DateTimeFormat(locale, options)` formats a date for people. The **locale** controls language and order; the **`timeZone`** option controls which local time is shown. `dateStyle`/`timeStyle` give quick presets; individual options (`weekday`, `month`, `hour`…) give fine control.
</En>
<Ur>
`Intl.DateTimeFormat(locale, options)` logon ke liye date format karta hai. **Locale** zaban aur tarteeb qaboo karti hai; **`timeZone`** option tay karta hai ke kaun sa local waqt dikhe. `dateStyle`/`timeStyle` jaldi wale presets dete hain; alag options (`weekday`, `month`, `hour`…) bareek qaboo dete hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const event = new Date("2026-11-12T14:30:00Z");
const opts = { dateStyle: "full", timeStyle: "short" };

for (const [locale, timeZone] of [
  ["en-US", "America/New_York"],
  ["en-GB", "Europe/London"],
  ["de-DE", "Europe/Berlin"],
  ["ja-JP", "Asia/Tokyo"],
]) {
  console.log(locale.padEnd(6), new Intl.DateTimeFormat(locale, { ...opts, timeZone }).format(event));
}

const custom = new Intl.DateTimeFormat("en-US", {
  weekday: "short",
  month: "short",
  day: "numeric",
  hour: "numeric",
  minute: "2-digit",
  timeZone: "America/Los_Angeles",
```

```javascript
  const seconds = Math.round((date - now) / 1000);

  }

  timeAgo(new Date("2026-11-09T12:00:00Z"), now),
  "|",
  timeAgo(new Date("2026-11-12T12:00:30Z"), now)
```

<DeepDive title="More about dates">

**Local vs UTC methods.** Each getter/setter has a local twin (`getHours` vs `getUTCHours`). The local ones use the runtime's time zone (the browser's, or the server's `TZ` setting). In servers, set `TZ=UTC` and still prefer explicit UTC methods.

**`getTimezoneOffset()`** returns the minutes **behind** UTC for that date in the local zone (positive west of UTC), and it can change with DST.

**Temporal** is the long-awaited replacement for `Date`, with separate types: `Temporal.Instant` (a moment), `PlainDate` (a calendar date), `PlainTime`, `ZonedDateTime` (moment + zone), and `Duration`. It does calendar math correctly, including DST and month ends. It's shipping in browsers; polyfills exist today.

**Libraries.** date-fns (functions on native Dates), Luxon (zones and durations), Day.js (small, Moment-like). Moment.js is in maintenance mode.

**Leap seconds** aren't represented: JavaScript time assumes every day has exactly 86,400 seconds (in UTC).

**Range.** A Date can represent about ±273,000 years around 1970; the 2038 problem of 32-bit seconds doesn't affect JavaScript's millisecond doubles.

**Databases.** PostgreSQL's `timestamptz` stores an instant (in UTC) and converts on input/output; `timestamp` without a zone stores wall-clock time and is a frequent source of bugs.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Real world: the deadline that moved">

A course platform stored assignment deadlines as local strings like `"2026-03-08T23:59"` and created them with `new Date(deadline)`. Students in Chicago saw the right time. Then two things happened.

First, the platform moved its servers to UTC. Reminder emails, built on the server with `new Date("2026-03-08T23:59")`, now interpreted the deadline as 23:59 **UTC**, which is 5:59 PM in Chicago, so reminders said "due in 2 hours" when students actually had 8.

Second, 8 March 2026 was the night US clocks sprang forward. A "deadline + 24 hours" grace period, added in milliseconds, landed at 00:59 local time instead of 23:59, briefly locking students out of late submissions.

The fix:

1. Store each deadline as a UTC instant **plus** the course's time zone (`America/Chicago`).
2. Convert teacher input ("March 8, 11:59 PM") to UTC using that zone, once, when saving.
3. Compute grace periods in the course's zone with a date library ("next day, same local time").
4. Format every displayed time with `Intl.DateTimeFormat` and an explicit `timeZone`, showing the zone abbreviation.

Lesson: **ambiguous local strings + server time zones + DST** is the classic recipe for date bugs. Be explicit about zones everywhere.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

- **Expiry checks belong on the server**, using the server's clock: tokens, password-reset links, coupons, trial ends. Never trust a client-sent "now".
- **Validate date input strictly** (format and range). Rejecting impossible dates and absurd years avoids crashes and logic errors.
- **Compare instants, not formatted strings.** `"2/1/2026" < "10/1/2026"` as text is wrong; compare `getTime()`.
- **Be careful with time zones in access rules** (store hours, exam windows): compute windows in the correct zone, or users in other zones can slip in early or late.
- **Clock skew between servers** can break short-lived tokens; allow a small leeway (for example 30–60 seconds) when validating `exp`/`nbf` claims.
- **Don't leak exact server times or zones** unnecessarily in error messages and headers.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="How does JavaScript represent dates internally?">
As a single number of milliseconds since the Unix epoch, 1 January 1970 00:00:00 UTC. The Date object has no time zone; local or UTC methods interpret that number for reading and display.
</QA>

<QA q="Why might new Date with a date-only string show the previous day?">
Date-only ISO strings are parsed as midnight UTC. Displayed in a time zone west of UTC, that moment falls on the previous calendar day.
</QA>

<QA q="How would you add one month to a date and what problems can occur?">
Copy the date and call setUTCMonth with getUTCMonth plus one. Months have different lengths, so dates like January 31 roll over into March; business logic often requires clamping to the last day of the month instead, which libraries or Temporal handle explicitly.
</QA>

<QA q="What is the best practice for storing and displaying dates in a web app?">
Store instants in UTC as ISO strings or timestamps, store the IANA time zone for users or local recurring events, perform calculations in UTC or the relevant zone, and format for display with Intl.DateTimeFormat using the viewer's locale and time zone.
</QA>

<QA q="What does Intl.RelativeTimeFormat do?">
It formats a numeric offset and unit into localized relative phrases such as 3 days ago or in 2 weeks, with numeric auto producing words like yesterday and tomorrow.
</QA>
