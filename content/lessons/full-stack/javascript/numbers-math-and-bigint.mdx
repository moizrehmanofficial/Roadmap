<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
JavaScript has **one main type for numbers**: `number`. It holds whole numbers (`42`) and decimals (`19.99`) alike, and you do math with `+ - * / % **`. Under the hood every `number` is stored in a fixed-size binary format, which has two famous side effects: some decimals can't be stored exactly (so `0.1 + 0.2` is `0.30000000000000004`), and whole numbers are only exact up to about **9 quadrillion** (`Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER`). For everyday work you handle this by **rounding when you display** and by **counting money in cents**. The built-in **`Math`** object gives you rounding, min and max, powers, square roots and random numbers. When you need **huge whole numbers** beyond the safe limit, use **`BigInt`**: write an `n` at the end, like `9007199254740993n`.
</En>
<Ur>
JavaScript me **numbers ki ek hi bari type** hai: `number`. Is me poore numbers (`42`) aur decimals (`19.99`) dono aate hain, aur hisaab `+ - * / % **` se hota hai. Andar har `number` ek tay size ke binary format me mehfooz hota hai, jis ke do mashhoor asraat hain: kuch decimals bilkul theek mehfooz nahi ho sakte (isliye `0.1 + 0.2` `0.30000000000000004` hai), aur poore numbers sirf taqreeban **9 quadrillion** tak bilkul durust hain (`Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER`). Roz ke kaam me aap ise **dikhate waqt round** kar ke aur **paison ko cents me gin kar** sambhalte hain. Bana banaya **`Math`** object rounding, min aur max, powers, square roots aur random numbers deta hai. Jab safe hadd se aage **bohot bare poore numbers** chahiyen, to **`BigInt`** lein: aakhir me `n` likhein, jaise `9007199254740993n`.
</Ur>
</Tr>

## Picture it

<Tr>
<En>

Picture a **calculator with a 16-digit screen**.

- It can show `0.30000000000000004` but not an infinitely long decimal, so tiny leftovers appear when fractions don't fit exactly, just like 1 ÷ 3 = 0.3333333333333333 on a real calculator.
- It can count whole numbers perfectly only up to the limit of its screen. Past that, the last digits get **rounded off** and different numbers start looking the same.
- **BigInt** is a **long strip of paper** instead of a screen: as long as you need, perfect for huge whole numbers, but it can't do decimals.
- And money? A shop counts **coins** (cents), not fractions of a dollar. Coins are whole numbers, and whole numbers are exact.

</En>
<Ur>

Ek **16 hindson wali screen ka calculator** sochein.

- Ye `0.30000000000000004` dikha sakta hai magar be-intiha lamba decimal nahi, isliye jab kasr bilkul fit na hon to chhote bache hue hisse nazar aate hain, bilkul jaise asli calculator par 1 ÷ 3 = 0.3333333333333333.
- Ye poore numbers ko sirf apni screen ki hadd tak bilkul theek gin sakta hai. Us ke baad aakhri hindse **round** ho jate hain aur mukhtalif numbers ek jaise lagne lagte hain.
- **BigInt** screen ke bajaye **kaghaz ki lambi patti** hai: jitni lambi chahiye, bohot bare poore numbers ke liye behtareen, magar decimals nahi kar sakta.
- Aur paise? Dukaan **sikke** (cents) ginti hai, dollar ke tukre nahi. Sikke poore numbers hain, aur poore numbers bilkul durust hote hain.

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Learn it step by step

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="Arithmetic operators">

<Tr>
<En>
The usual math symbols work as you'd expect. Two you may not know: `%` gives the **remainder** after dividing, and `**` means "to the power of".
</En>
<Ur>
Aam hisaab ke nishaan waise hi chalte hain jaise aap umeed karte hain. Do shayad naye hon: `%` taqseem ke baad **baqi** deta hai, aur `**` ka matlab "ki power".
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const price = 12;
const qty = 3;

console.log("subtotal:", price * qty);
console.log("split 3 ways:", 100 / 3);
console.log("17 % 5 =", 17 % 5);
console.log("is 8 even?", 8 % 2 === 0);
console.log("2 ** 10 =", 2 ** 10);
console.log("1_000_000 =", 1_000_000);
```

```text output
subtotal: 36
split 3 ways: 33.333333333333336
17 % 5 = 2
is 8 even? true
2 ** 10 = 1024
1_000_000 = 1000000
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `100 / 3` gives a decimal: there's no separate "integer division" in JavaScript. Use `Math.floor(100 / 3)` if you want `33`.
- `17 % 5` is `2` because 5 goes into 17 three times (15) with **2 left over**. `% 2 === 0` is the classic "is it even?" check.
- Underscores in `1_000_000` are just for readability; the value is one million.
- Shortcuts: `total += 5` means `total = total + 5`; `count++` adds 1; `count--` subtracts 1.

</En>
<Ur>

- `100 / 3` decimal deta hai: JavaScript me alag "poori taqseem" nahi. `33` chahiye to `Math.floor(100 / 3)` lein.
- `17 % 5` `2` hai kyun ke 5, 17 me teen dafa jata hai (15) aur **2 bachte hain**. `% 2 === 0` mashhoor "kya ye juft hai?" check hai.
- `1_000_000` ke underscores sirf parhne me aasani ke liye hain; value das lakh hai.
- Shortcuts: `total += 5` ka matlab `total = total + 5`; `count++` 1 jorta hai; `count--` 1 ghatata hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Order of operations">

<Tr>
<En>
Like school math, `**` happens first, then `*`, `/` and `%`, then `+` and `-`. Operations at the same level go left to right. **Brackets** override everything, and they make your intent clear to readers too.
</En>
<Ur>
School ke hisaab ki tarah pehle `**`, phir `*`, `/` aur `%`, phir `+` aur `-`. Ek hi level ke amal baayen se daayen chalte hain. **Brackets** sab par bhaari hain, aur parhne walon ko aap ki niyat bhi wazeh karte hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const price = 20;
const qty = 2;
const shipping = 5;

console.log(price * qty + shipping);
console.log(price * (qty + shipping));
console.log(2 + 3 * 4 ** 2);
console.log(100 - 10 - 5);

const discountPercent = 10;
const total = price * qty * (1 - discountPercent / 100) + shipping;
console.log("total:", total);
```

```text output
45
140
50
85
total: 41
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `price * qty + shipping` = 40 + 5 = **45**. With brackets, `price * (qty + shipping)` = 20 × 7 = **140**. Same numbers, very different answer.
- `2 + 3 * 4 ** 2`: first `4 ** 2` = 16, then `3 * 16` = 48, then `2 + 48` = **50**.
- `100 - 10 - 5` goes left to right: 90 − 5 = 85.
- The total line reads naturally with brackets: 40 × 0.9 = 36, plus 5 shipping = **41**.

</En>
<Ur>

- `price * qty + shipping` = 40 + 5 = **45**. Brackets ke saath `price * (qty + shipping)` = 20 × 7 = **140**. Wahi numbers, bilkul mukhtalif jawab.
- `2 + 3 * 4 ** 2`: pehle `4 ** 2` = 16, phir `3 * 16` = 48, phir `2 + 48` = **50**.
- `100 - 10 - 5` baayen se daayen: 90 − 5 = 85.
- Total wali line brackets ke saath aasaani se parhi jati hai: 40 × 0.9 = 36, plus 5 shipping = **41**.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Why 0.1 + 0.2 isn't 0.3">

<Tr>
<En>
Computers store numbers in **binary** (base 2). Just as 1/3 can't be written exactly in decimal (0.3333…), 0.1 can't be written exactly in binary. The stored values are a tiny bit off, and sometimes you can see it.
</En>
<Ur>
Computers numbers **binary** (base 2) me mehfooz karte hain. Jaise 1/3 decimal me bilkul theek nahi likha ja sakta (0.3333…), waise hi 0.1 binary me bilkul theek nahi likha ja sakta. Mehfooz values zara si hati hoti hain, aur kabhi kabhi ye nazar aa jata hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
console.log(0.1 + 0.2);
console.log(0.1 + 0.2 === 0.3);
console.log(19.99 * 3);
console.log(1.15 * 100);

// Close enough? Compare with a tiny tolerance
const a = 0.1 + 0.2;
console.log(Math.abs(a - 0.3) < Number.EPSILON);
```

```text output
0.30000000000000004
false
59.97
114.99999999999999
true
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- The error is tiny (0.00000000000000004), but `=== 0.3` is **false**, which breaks comparisons.
- `19.99 * 3` happened to come out clean, while `1.15 * 100` didn't. You can't predict which ones will, so **don't rely on decimals being exact**.
- To compare decimals, check whether they're **close enough**: `Math.abs(a - b) < Number.EPSILON` (a tiny number, about 2 × 10⁻¹⁶). For money, there's a better way: next step.
- This isn't a JavaScript bug. Python, Java, C# and Excel use the same IEEE 754 format and do the same thing.

</En>
<Ur>

- Ghalti bohot chhoti hai (0.00000000000000004), magar `=== 0.3` **false** hai, jo muqablon ko tor deta hai.
- `19.99 * 3` saaf nikla, jabke `1.15 * 100` nahi. Aap andaza nahi laga sakte kaun sa saaf niklega, isliye **decimals ke bilkul theek hone par bharosa na karein**.
- Decimals ka muqabla karne ke liye check karein ke wo **kaafi qareeb** hain: `Math.abs(a - b) < Number.EPSILON` (ek bohot chhota number, taqreeban 2 × 10⁻¹⁶). Paison ke liye behtar tareeqa hai: agla qadam.
- Ye JavaScript ka bug nahi. Python, Java, C# aur Excel wahi IEEE 754 format istemal karte hain aur yahi karte hain.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Money: count in cents">

<Tr>
<En>
Whole numbers are exact. So store and add money as **integer cents**, and only divide by 100 when you show it. This is what payment providers like Stripe do: `1999` means $19.99.
</En>
<Ur>
Poore numbers bilkul durust hote hain. Isliye paise **poore cents** me rakhein aur jorein, aur sirf dikhate waqt 100 se taqseem karein. Payment providers jaise Stripe yahi karte hain: `1999` ka matlab $19.99.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
// Wrong: dollars as decimals
const pricesInDollars = [1.1, 4.35, 19.99];
let wrongTotal = 0;
for (const p of pricesInDollars) wrongTotal += p;
console.log("dollars:", wrongTotal);

// Right: cents as integers
const pricesInCents = [110, 435, 1999];
let totalCents = 0;
for (const c of pricesInCents) totalCents += c;
console.log("cents:", totalCents);
console.log("display:", "$" + (totalCents / 100).toFixed(2));

// 8.25% tax, rounded to the nearest cent
const taxCents = Math.round(totalCents * 0.0825);
console.log("tax cents:", taxCents);
```

```text output
dollars: 25.439999999999998
cents: 2544
display: $25.44
tax cents: 210
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- Adding dollar decimals gave `25.439999999999998`. Adding cents gave exactly `2544`.
- `(2544 / 100).toFixed(2)` turns it into the **text** `"25.44"` for display. `toFixed(2)` always shows exactly two decimals, so 3.5 would show as `"3.50"`.
- When a calculation produces fractions of a cent (tax: 2544 × 0.0825 = 209.88), **round once, right there**, back to whole cents.
- Convert user input like `"19.99"` to cents with `Math.round(Number(input) * 100)`; the `Math.round` fixes cases like `1.15 * 100`.

</En>
<Ur>

- Dollar ke decimals jorne se `25.439999999999998` aaya. Cents jorne se bilkul `2544`.
- `(2544 / 100).toFixed(2)` use dikhane ke liye **text** `"25.44"` me badalta hai. `toFixed(2)` hamesha bilkul do decimals dikhata hai, isliye 3.5 `"3.50"` dikhega.
- Jab hisaab cent ke tukre banaye (tax: 2544 × 0.0825 = 209.88), **wahin, ek dafa round karein**, wapas poore cents me.
- User ke input jaise `"19.99"` ko `Math.round(Number(input) * 100)` se cents me badlein; `Math.round` `1.15 * 100` jaise masail theek karta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Rounding: four ways">

<Tr>
<En>
`Math.round` goes to the **nearest** whole number, `Math.floor` always **down**, `Math.ceil` always **up**, and `Math.trunc` just **cuts off** the decimals. They differ most with negative numbers.
</En>
<Ur>
`Math.round` **qareebi** poore number par jata hai, `Math.floor` hamesha **neeche**, `Math.ceil` hamesha **upar**, aur `Math.trunc` bas decimals **kaat deta** hai. Manfi numbers ke saath in me sab se zyada farq hota hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
for (const n of [4.4, 4.5, 4.6, -4.4, -4.5, -4.6]) {
  console.log(n, "round:", Math.round(n), "floor:", Math.floor(n), "ceil:", Math.ceil(n), "trunc:", Math.trunc(n));
}

console.log((2.345).toFixed(2), typeof (2.345).toFixed(2));
console.log((1.005).toFixed(2));
console.log(Math.round(2.345 * 100) / 100);
```

```text output
4.4 round: 4 floor: 4 ceil: 5 trunc: 4
4.5 round: 5 floor: 4 ceil: 5 trunc: 4
4.6 round: 5 floor: 4 ceil: 5 trunc: 4
-4.4 round: -4 floor: -5 ceil: -4 trunc: -4
-4.5 round: -4 floor: -5 ceil: -4 trunc: -4
-4.6 round: -5 floor: -5 ceil: -4 trunc: -4
2.35 string
1.00
2.35
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `Math.round(-4.5)` is **-4**, not -5: JavaScript rounds .5 **up** (toward positive infinity).
- `floor` on a negative goes further from zero (-4.4 → -5); `trunc` goes toward zero (-4.4 → -4).
- `toFixed(2)` returns a **string**, meant for display. And it inherits floating-point surprises: `(1.005).toFixed(2)` is `"1.00"`, because 1.005 is really stored as 1.00499999….
- Common uses: `Math.ceil(items / perPage)` for the number of pages; `Math.floor` for "full boxes"; `Math.round` for cents.

</En>
<Ur>

- `Math.round(-4.5)` **-4** hai, -5 nahi: JavaScript .5 ko **upar** (positive infinity ki taraf) round karti hai.
- Manfi par `floor` zero se door jata hai (-4.4 → -5); `trunc` zero ki taraf (-4.4 → -4).
- `toFixed(2)` **string** wapas karta hai, dikhane ke liye. Aur floating-point ki hairaniyan bhi saath laata hai: `(1.005).toFixed(2)` `"1.00"` hai, kyun ke 1.005 asal me 1.00499999… mehfooz hai.
- Aam istemal: pages ki tadaad ke liye `Math.ceil(items / perPage)`; "poore dabbon" ke liye `Math.floor`; cents ke liye `Math.round`.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Turning text into numbers">

<Tr>
<En>
Form inputs, URL query values and many API fields arrive as **text**. `Number(text)` converts the whole text and gives `NaN` if any part isn't a number. `parseInt` and `parseFloat` read a number from the **start** of the text and stop at the first character that doesn't fit.
</En>
<Ur>
Form inputs, URL query values aur API ki bohot si fields **text** ki shakal me aati hain. `Number(text)` poora text badalta hai aur agar koi hissa number na ho to `NaN` deta hai. `parseInt` aur `parseFloat` text ke **shuru** se number parhte hain aur pehle na-munasib character par ruk jate hain.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
console.log(Number("42"), Number("3.5"), Number("  7  "));
console.log(Number("12px"), Number("abc"));
console.log(Number(""), Number(null), Number(true));

console.log(parseInt("12px"), parseFloat("3.5kg"), parseInt("3.9"));
console.log(parseInt("px12"), parseInt("ff", 16), parseInt("101", 2));

console.log("2" + "3", Number("2") + Number("3"));
```

```text output
42 3.5 7
NaN NaN
0 0 1
12 3.5 3
NaN 255 5
23 5
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `Number` is **strict**: `"12px"` → `NaN`. That's usually what you want for form input: bad input should fail loudly.
- Watch out: `Number("")` is **0**, not `NaN`. An empty form field silently becomes zero. Check for empty text first.
- `parseInt` is **lenient**: it reads `12` from `"12px"` and ignores the rest, and it **drops decimals** (`"3.9"` → 3). Useful for CSS values; risky for user input.
- `parseInt(text, base)` reads other bases: hex (`16`) and binary (`2`).
- Text plus text **joins** (`"23"`); convert first, then add (`5`).

</En>
<Ur>

- `Number` **sakht** hai: `"12px"` → `NaN`. Form input ke liye aam taur par yahi chahiye: ghalat input zor se fail hona chahiye.
- Ehtiyat: `Number("")` **0** hai, `NaN` nahi. Khaali form field khamoshi se zero ban jati hai. Pehle khaali text check karein.
- `parseInt` **narm** hai: `"12px"` se `12` parh kar baqi chhor deta hai, aur **decimals gira deta** hai (`"3.9"` → 3). CSS values ke liye kaam ka; user input ke liye khatarnak.
- `parseInt(text, base)` doosre bases parhta hai: hex (`16`) aur binary (`2`).
- Text plus text **jorta** hai (`"23"`); pehle badlein, phir jorein (`5`).

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="NaN and Infinity">

<Tr>
<En>
**`NaN`** ("Not a Number") is the result of math that makes no sense, like `Number("abc")` or `0 / 0`. It spreads: any math with `NaN` gives `NaN`. **`Infinity`** comes from dividing by zero or going beyond the largest number.
</En>
<Ur>
**`NaN`** ("Not a Number") aise hisaab ka nateeja hai jo be-maani ho, jaise `Number("abc")` ya `0 / 0`. Ye phailta hai: `NaN` ke saath har hisaab `NaN` deta hai. **`Infinity`** zero se taqseem karne ya sab se bare number se aage jane se aata hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const qty = Number("two");
console.log(qty, typeof qty);
console.log(qty * 10 + 5);
console.log(qty === NaN, Number.isNaN(qty));

console.log(isNaN("hello"), Number.isNaN("hello"));

console.log(10 / 0, -10 / 0, 0 / 0);
console.log(Number.isFinite(42), Number.isFinite(Infinity), Number.isFinite(NaN));
```

```text output
NaN number
NaN
false true
true false
Infinity -Infinity NaN
true false false
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `typeof NaN` is `"number"`: it's a special number value meaning "this calculation failed".
- One bad input poisoned the whole total: `NaN * 10 + 5` is still `NaN`. That's how a cart ends up showing "$NaN".
- **`NaN === NaN` is false.** It's the only value not equal to itself, so you must test with **`Number.isNaN(x)`**.
- The old global `isNaN("hello")` first converts its argument to a number, so it says `true` for any non-numeric text. `Number.isNaN` doesn't convert, so it's the reliable one.
- `Number.isFinite(x)` is the best "is this a real, usable number?" check: false for `NaN`, `Infinity` and non-numbers.

</En>
<Ur>

- `typeof NaN` `"number"` hai: ye khaas number value hai jis ka matlab "ye hisaab nakaam hua".
- Ek ghalat input ne poora total zehreela kar diya: `NaN * 10 + 5` ab bhi `NaN` hai. Isi tarah cart "$NaN" dikhane lagta hai.
- **`NaN === NaN` false hai.** Ye wahid value hai jo apne aap ke barabar nahi, isliye test **`Number.isNaN(x)`** se karna zaroori hai.
- Purana global `isNaN("hello")` pehle argument ko number me badalta hai, isliye har ghair numeric text par `true` kehta hai. `Number.isNaN` nahi badalta, isliye wahi qabil-e-bharosa hai.
- `Number.isFinite(x)` "kya ye asli, kaam ka number hai?" ka behtareen check hai: `NaN`, `Infinity` aur ghair numbers par false.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="The Math toolbox">

<Tr>
<En>
`Math` is a built-in object full of helpers. You don't create it; just call `Math.something(…)`.
</En>
<Ur>
`Math` ek bana banaya object hai jo madadgaron se bhara hai. Ise banana nahi parta; bas `Math.something(…)` call karein.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const scores = [72, 95, 88, 61];

console.log("max:", Math.max(...scores), "min:", Math.min(...scores));
console.log("abs:", Math.abs(-15));
console.log("sqrt:", Math.sqrt(144), "cbrt:", Math.cbrt(27));
console.log("pow:", Math.pow(2, 8), "PI:", Math.PI);
console.log("hypot:", Math.hypot(3, 4));

// Keep a value inside a range (clamp)
const clamp = (value, min, max) => Math.min(Math.max(value, min), max);
console.log(clamp(150, 0, 100), clamp(-5, 0, 100), clamp(42, 0, 100));

console.log(Math.max(), Math.max(1, "nope"));
```

```text output

abs: 15
sqrt: 12 cbrt: 3
pow: 256 PI: 3.141592653589793
hypot: 5
100 0 42
-Infinity NaN
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `Math.max` and `Math.min` take separate numbers, not an array. The `...` (spread) unpacks the array into separate arguments.
- `clamp` is a handy pattern for things like volume, progress bars and quantities: never below `min`, never above `max`.
- With no arguments, `Math.max()` is `-Infinity` (anything is bigger). Any non-number argument makes it `NaN`. Check your data first.

</En>
<Ur>

- `Math.max` aur `Math.min` alag alag numbers lete hain, array nahi. `...` (spread) array ko alag arguments me khol deta hai.
- `clamp` volume, progress bars aur quantities jaisi cheezon ke liye kaam ka pattern hai: kabhi `min` se kam nahi, kabhi `max` se zyada nahi.
- Baghair arguments ke `Math.max()` `-Infinity` hai (har cheez bari hai). Koi bhi ghair number argument ise `NaN` bana deta hai. Pehle apna data check karein.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Random numbers in a range">

<Tr>
<En>
`Math.random()` gives a decimal from **0 up to (but not including) 1**. To get a whole number between `min` and `max`, scale it, then floor it. Because the results change every run, this example checks properties instead of printing exact numbers.
</En>
<Ur>
`Math.random()` **0 se 1 tak (1 shamil nahi)** ek decimal deta hai. `min` aur `max` ke darmiyan poora number lene ke liye ise phailayen, phir floor karein. Chunke nataij har dafa badalte hain, ye namoona bilkul theek numbers print karne ke bajaye khoobiyan check karta hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
// Whole number from min to max, both included
function randomInt(min, max) {
  return Math.floor(Math.random() * (max - min + 1)) + min;
}

const rolls = [];
for (let i = 0; i < 10000; i++) rolls.push(randomInt(1, 6));

console.log("all whole numbers?", rolls.every(Number.isInteger));
console.log("smallest:", Math.min(...rolls), "largest:", Math.max(...rolls));
console.log("ever 7?", rolls.includes(7));
```

```text output
all whole numbers? true
smallest: 1 largest: 6
ever 7? false
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `Math.random() * 6` is 0 to 5.999…; `Math.floor` makes it 0–5; `+ 1` makes it **1–6**. The formula `max - min + 1` is the number of possible values.
- 10,000 dice rolls hit both 1 and 6 and never 7, so both ends are included and nothing goes outside.
- **`Math.random()` is not secure.** Never use it for passwords, tokens or anything an attacker could guess. Use `crypto.getRandomValues` or `crypto.randomUUID()` instead.

</En>
<Ur>

- `Math.random() * 6` 0 se 5.999… hai; `Math.floor` ise 0–5 banata hai; `+ 1` ise **1–6** banata hai. Formula `max - min + 1` mumkina values ki tadaad hai.
- 10,000 dice rolls ne 1 aur 6 dono chhue aur kabhi 7 nahi, isliye dono kinare shamil hain aur kuch bahar nahi gaya.
- **`Math.random()` mehfooz nahi.** Ise passwords, tokens ya kisi aisi cheez ke liye kabhi istemal na karein jis ka andaza hamla awar laga sake. Is ke bajaye `crypto.getRandomValues` ya `crypto.randomUUID()` lein.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="The largest safe whole number">

<Tr>
<En>
A `number` can store whole numbers **exactly** only up to `Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER` (9,007,199,254,740,991, which is 2⁵³ − 1). Beyond that, some whole numbers can't be represented, and JavaScript quietly rounds them to a neighbour.
</En>
<Ur>
`number` poore numbers ko sirf `Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER` (9,007,199,254,740,991, yani 2⁵³ − 1) tak **bilkul theek** rakh sakta hai. Us ke baad kuch poore numbers ban hi nahi sakte, aur JavaScript khamoshi se unhe padosi number par round kar deti hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const max = Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER;
console.log(max);
console.log(max + 1);
console.log(max + 2);
console.log(max + 1 === max + 2);

console.log(Number.isSafeInteger(max), Number.isSafeInteger(max + 1));

// A 19-digit order id from an API, read as a number
const fromApi = JSON.parse('{"orderId": 1234567890123456789}');
console.log(fromApi.orderId);
```

```text output
9007199254740991
9007199254740992
9007199254740992
true
true false
1234567890123456800
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `max + 2` should be ...993, but it printed ...992, the **same** as `max + 1`. Two different numbers became equal.
- The real danger: an API sent the id `1234567890123456789`, and `JSON.parse` turned it into `1234567890123456800`. Looking up or updating that order would hit the **wrong record or none**.
- Fixes: send big ids as **strings** in JSON (`"orderId": "1234567890123456789"`), which is what Twitter's and many other APIs do, or use `BigInt` (next step).

</En>
<Ur>

- `max + 2` ko ...993 hona chahiye tha, magar ...992 print hua, `max + 1` ke **barabar**. Do mukhtalif numbers barabar ho gaye.
- Asal khatra: API ne id `1234567890123456789` bheji, aur `JSON.parse` ne use `1234567890123456800` bana diya. Us order ko dhoondna ya update karna **ghalat record ya koi nahi** chhooega.
- Hal: bari ids JSON me **strings** ki shakal me bhejein (`"orderId": "1234567890123456789"`), jo Twitter ki aur bohot si APIs karti hain, ya `BigInt` lein (agla qadam).

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="BigInt for huge whole numbers">

<Tr>
<En>
A **BigInt** is a whole number of any size. Write `n` after the digits, or call `BigInt("…")` with a string. BigInts do exact math, but they **can't be mixed** with normal numbers and they have **no decimals**.
</En>
<Ur>
**BigInt** kisi bhi size ka poora number hai. Hindson ke baad `n` likhein, ya string ke saath `BigInt("…")` call karein. BigInts bilkul theek hisaab karte hain, magar normal numbers ke saath **mil nahi sakte** aur in me **decimals nahi** hote.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```javascript
const id = BigInt("1234567890123456789");
console.log(id, typeof id);
console.log(id + 1n);
console.log(2n ** 64n);
console.log(7n / 2n);

try {
  console.log(id + 1);
} catch (error) {
  console.log(error.name + ":", error.message);
}

console.log(Number(10n) + 1, BigInt(5) * 3n);
console.log(10n > 9, 10n == 10, 10n === 10);
console.log(id.toString());
```

```text output
1234567890123456789n bigint
1234567890123456790n
18446744073709551616n
3n
TypeError: Cannot mix BigInt and other types, use explicit conversions
11 15n
true true false
1234567890123456789
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `BigInt("1234567890123456789")` kept every digit, and `+ 1n` gave exactly ...790. `2n ** 64n` is 18,446,744,073,709,551,616, far beyond the safe number limit.
- `7n / 2n` is `3n`: BigInt division **drops the fraction**.
- `id + 1` threw a `TypeError`. You must convert one side on purpose: `Number(big)` (may lose precision) or `BigInt(n)`.
- Comparisons work across types (`10n > 9`, `10n == 10`), but `===` is false because the types differ.
- `JSON.stringify` can't handle BigInt (it throws). Convert with `.toString()` before sending it as JSON.

</En>
<Ur>

- `BigInt("1234567890123456789")` ne har hindsa mehfooz rakha, aur `+ 1n` ne bilkul ...790 diya. `2n ** 64n` 18,446,744,073,709,551,616 hai, safe hadd se kaheen aage.
- `7n / 2n` `3n` hai: BigInt ki taqseem **kasr gira deti** hai.
- `id + 1` ne `TypeError` phenka. Ek taraf ko jaan boojh kar badalna parta hai: `Number(big)` (durustagi kho sakti hai) ya `BigInt(n)`.
- Muqable types ke paar chalte hain (`10n > 9`, `10n == 10`), magar `===` false hai kyun ke types mukhtalif hain.
- `JSON.stringify` BigInt nahi sambhal sakta (error phenkta hai). JSON me bhejne se pehle `.toString()` se badlein.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

`toLocaleString` aur `Intl.NumberFormat` hazaron ke separators, currency ke nishaan, percentages aur "1.2K" jaisi mukhtasar shaklen jorte hain, sab ek **locale** (zaban aur ilaqa) ke qawaid ke mutabiq.

- Wahi number US me `1,234,567.891` aur Germany me `1.234.567,891` likha jata hai. Khud commas lagane ke bajaye `Intl` ko sambhalne dein.

<Challenge title="Safe quantity input">

<Tr>
<En>
Write `parseQuantity(text)` that returns a whole number from 1 to 99, or `null` for anything else (empty text, letters, decimals, 0, negatives, 100+). Test it with `"3"`, `""`, `"2.5"`, `"abc"`, `"0"`, `"150"`, `" 7 "`.
</En>
<Ur>
`parseQuantity(text)` likhein jo 1 se 99 tak poora number wapas kare, ya kisi bhi aur cheez (khaali text, huroof, decimals, 0, manfi, 100+) par `null`. Ise `"3"`, `""`, `"2.5"`, `"abc"`, `"0"`, `"150"`, `" 7 "` se test karein.
</Ur>

<Solution>

```javascript
function parseQuantity(text) {
  if (text.trim() === "") return null;
  const n = Number(text);
  if (!Number.isInteger(n) || n < 1 || n > 99) return null;
  return n;
}

for (const t of ["3", "", "2.5", "abc", "0", "150", " 7 "]) {
  console.log(JSON.stringify(t), "->", parseQuantity(t));
}
```

```text output
"2.5" -> null
"abc" -> null
"0" -> null
"150" -> null
" 7 " -> 7
```

<En>

</En>
<Ur>
Khaali check pehle aata hai kyun ke `Number("")` 0 hai. `Number.isInteger` `NaN`, `Infinity` aur decimals ek hi dafa me mustarad kar deta hai.

## Remember

<Takeaways>
<Tr>
<En>

- One `number` type for integers and decimals; operators `+ - * / % **`, `*` before `+`, brackets to be clear.
- Decimals are slightly imprecise (`0.1 + 0.2`). Compare with a tolerance; **store money as integer cents**.
- Rounding: `Math.round` (nearest, .5 up), `floor` (down), `ceil` (up), `trunc` (cut). `toFixed` returns **text**.
- Convert input with `Number()` (strict; `""` → 0!), `parseInt`/`parseFloat` (lenient).
- `NaN` spreads and isn't equal to itself: use `Number.isNaN` / `Number.isFinite`.
- `Math`: `max`, `min`, `abs`, `sqrt`, `random`. `Math.random()` isn't secure.
- Whole numbers are exact only up to `Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER`; use **strings or BigInt** for big ids.
- Show numbers with `Intl.NumberFormat` (currency, percent, compact, locale separators).

</En>
<Ur>

- Integers aur decimals ke liye ek `number` type; operators `+ - * / % **`, `+` se pehle `*`, wazeh karne ke liye brackets.
- Decimals thore ghair durust hain (`0.1 + 0.2`). Tolerance se muqabla karein; **paise poore cents me rakhein**.
- Rounding: `Math.round` (qareebi, .5 upar), `floor` (neeche), `ceil` (upar), `trunc` (kaato). `toFixed` **text** deta hai.
- Input `Number()` (sakht; `""` → 0!), `parseInt`/`parseFloat` (narm) se badlein.
- `NaN` phailta hai aur apne barabar nahi: `Number.isNaN` / `Number.isFinite` lein.
- `Math`: `max`, `min`, `abs`, `sqrt`, `random`. `Math.random()` mehfooz nahi.
- Poore numbers sirf `Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER` tak durust hain; bari ids ke liye **strings ya BigInt**.
- Numbers `Intl.NumberFormat` se dikhayen (currency, percent, compact, locale separators).

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Takeaways>

<DeepDive title="How numbers are stored">

**IEEE 754 double precision.** Every `number` is 64 bits: 1 sign bit, 11 exponent bits and 52 fraction bits (plus an implied leading 1, so 53 bits of precision). That's why integers are exact up to 2⁵³ and decimals have about 15–17 significant digits.

**Special values.** `Infinity`, `-Infinity`, `NaN`, and even `-0` (`Object.is(-0, 0)` is false, `1 / -0` is `-Infinity`).

**Other literals.** Hex `0xff` (255), binary `0b1010` (10), octal `0o17` (15), exponent `1.5e3` (1500).

**Number methods.** `toString(2)` writes binary, `toPrecision(3)` gives 3 significant digits, `Number.parseFloat` is the same as `parseFloat`.

**Bitwise operators** (`&`, `|`, `^`, `<<`, `>>`) work on 32-bit integers; `x | 0` is an old trick for truncating, but it breaks above 2³¹, so prefer `Math.trunc`.

**Decimal libraries.** For accounting, tax or scientific work with exact decimals, use `decimal.js` or `big.js`, or a database `NUMERIC` column, and do the precise math there.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Real world: the order ids that changed">

An online store moved its database to a system that generated 64-bit order ids like `1435793561271533569`. The API returned them as JSON numbers, and the admin dashboard (in JavaScript) parsed them with `JSON.parse`.

Support staff started reporting that clicking "Refund" sometimes refunded **the wrong customer's order**. The ids in the dashboard looked right at a glance, but the last few digits were different from the database: JavaScript had rounded every id above 2⁵³ to the nearest representable number, and two neighbouring orders sometimes collapsed into the same id.

The fix:

1. The API changed to send ids as strings: `"id": "1435793561271533569"`.
2. The dashboard treated ids as opaque text everywhere: never doing math on them, only comparing and sending them back.
3. A test now creates an order with an id above `Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER` and checks that the dashboard round-trips it exactly.

Lesson: **ids aren't numbers you calculate with.** If they can be large, keep them as strings (or BigInt) from the moment they leave the database.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

- **Validate numeric input on the server**: type, whole or decimal, minimum and maximum. A quantity of `-5` or `1e308` should be rejected, not multiplied by a price.
- **Never trust totals sent by the client.** Recalculate prices and totals on the server from your own data.
- **Watch for `NaN` and `Infinity`** sneaking into calculations and comparisons: `NaN > 100` and `NaN < 100` are both false, which can skip a limit check entirely. Use `Number.isFinite` first.
- **Use `crypto.getRandomValues` / `crypto.randomUUID()`** for tokens, codes and ids; `Math.random()` is predictable.
- **Big ids as strings**, so rounding can't make one user's record point to another's.
- **Integer overflow in other systems**: if you send a huge number to a database column or another service, it may wrap or fail there. Check limits on both sides.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="Why does 0.1 + 0.2 not equal 0.3 in JavaScript?">
Numbers are IEEE 754 double-precision binary floating point values, and 0.1 and 0.2 have no exact binary representation, so their stored approximations add up to a value slightly above 0.3. Compare with a tolerance or use integers such as cents.
</QA>

<QA q="What is Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER and why does it matter?">
It is 2 to the power of 53 minus 1, the largest integer that can be represented exactly along with every integer below it. Beyond it, distinct integers can map to the same value, which corrupts ids and counters, so large integers should be handled as strings or BigInt.
</QA>

<QA q="What is the difference between isNaN and Number.isNaN?">
The global isNaN converts its argument to a number first, so non-numeric strings return true. Number.isNaN returns true only if the value is actually NaN, without conversion.
</QA>

<QA q="What is the difference between Number() and parseInt()?">
Number converts the entire string and returns NaN if any part is invalid, treating an empty string as 0. parseInt reads digits from the start until it hits an invalid character, ignores the rest, drops decimals and accepts a radix.
</QA>

<QA q="What are the limitations of BigInt?">
BigInt represents only integers, cannot be mixed with numbers in arithmetic without explicit conversion, is not supported by Math methods, and cannot be serialized by JSON.stringify without custom handling.
</QA>
