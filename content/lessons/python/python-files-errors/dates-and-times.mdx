<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
Python's **`datetime`** module has three types you'll use constantly: **`date`** (a calendar day), **`datetime`** (a day plus a time) and **`timedelta`** (a duration). Subtracting two dates gives a timedelta, and adding a timedelta moves a date, so "due in 30 days" or "how long did this job take" are one line each. To turn times into text use **`strftime`** with format codes like `%Y-%m-%d %H:%M`; to read text use **`strptime`** with the same codes, or **`fromisoformat`** for ISO 8601 strings such as `2026-11-23T18:05:00+00:00`. The hard part is **time zones**. A datetime without time zone information is **naive**: "9:00" but nobody knows where. An **aware** datetime carries a zone or offset. The rule professionals follow: **store and compute in UTC**, attach real zones with **`zoneinfo.ZoneInfo("America/New_York")`**, and convert to local time only for display. Zones have **daylight saving time**, so the same wall-clock time has different UTC offsets in summer and winter, and "one day later" can be 23 or 25 hours. Finally, code that calls `now()` everywhere is hard to test; pass the current time in as a parameter.
</En>
<Ur>
Python ke **`datetime`** module me teen types hain jo aap barabar istemal karenge: **`date`** (calendar ka din), **`datetime`** (din aur waqt) aur **`timedelta`** (muddat). Do dates ghatane se timedelta milta, aur timedelta jorne se date aage barhti hai, isliye "30 din me due" ya "ye job kitni der chali" ek ek line ke kaam hain. Waqt ko text banane ke liye `%Y-%m-%d %H:%M` jaise format codes ke saath **`strftime`** lein; text parhne ke liye unhi codes ke saath **`strptime`**, ya `2026-11-23T18:05:00+00:00` jaisi ISO 8601 strings ke liye **`fromisoformat`**. Mushkil hissa **time zones** hain. Time zone maloomat ke baghair datetime **naive** hai: "9:00" magar kahan ka, koi nahi janta. **Aware** datetime zone ya offset rakhta hai. Professionals ka qaida: **UTC me store aur hisaab karein**, asal zones **`zoneinfo.ZoneInfo("America/New_York")`** se lagayen, aur sirf dikhane ke liye local waqt me badlein. Zones me **daylight saving time** hota hai, isliye wahi ghari ka waqt garmi aur sardi me mukhtalif UTC offsets rakhta hai, aur "ek din baad" 23 ya 25 ghante ho sakta hai. Aakhir me, har jagah `now()` bulane wala code test karna mushkil hai; mojooda waqt parameter ke taur par andar dein.
</Ur>

## Picture it

<Tr>
<En>

Think of **airline schedules**.

- Departure and arrival boards show **local time** at each airport, but the airline's systems plan everything in **UTC** so that a flight from New York to Tokyo doesn't "arrive before it left".
- A naive datetime is a boarding pass that says "09:00" with no city printed on it.
- Daylight saving time is a city deciding to move its clocks an hour twice a year; a flight that always leaves at "12:00 local" is not always 24 hours after yesterday's.
- `timedelta` is the flight duration: it doesn't care about time zones, only elapsed time.

</En>
<Ur>

**Airline ke schedules** socheyn.

- Rawangi aur aamad ke boards har airport ka **local waqt** dikhate hain, magar airline ke systems sab kuch **UTC** me plan karte hain taa ke New York se Tokyo ki flight "chalne se pehle pohanch" na jaye.
- Naive datetime aisa boarding pass hai jis par "09:00" likha ho magar shehar ka naam na ho.
- Daylight saving time kisi shehar ka saal me do dafa apni ghariyan ek ghanta hilane ka faisla hai; hamesha "12:00 local" par chalne wali flight hamesha kal wali se 24 ghante baad nahi hoti.
- `timedelta` flight ki muddat hai: use time zones ki parwah nahi, sirf guzra waqt.

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Learn it step by step

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="date, datetime, timedelta, formatting and parsing">

<Tr>
<En>
`dates.py` builds a date and a datetime, reads their parts, does arithmetic with `timedelta` (a due date, a span across midnight, and "Jan 31 + 30 days"), formats a datetime three ways, parses text with `strptime` and `fromisoformat`, catches an invalid date, and compares and sorts dates.
</En>
<Ur>
`dates.py` date aur datetime banati, un ke hisse parhti, `timedelta` se hisaab karti (due date, aadhi raat ke paar muddat, aur "Jan 31 + 30 din"), datetime ko teen tareeqon se format karti, `strptime` aur `fromisoformat` se text parse karti, ghalat date pakarti, aur dates ka muqabla aur sort karti hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
cat > dates.py <<'EOF'
from datetime import date, datetime, timedelta

d = date(2026, 11, 23)
print(d, d.year, d.month, d.day, d.strftime("%A"), d.isoweekday(), d.isoformat())

t = datetime(2026, 11, 23, 9, 30, 15)
print(t, "| date:", t.date(), "| time:", t.time(), "| replace:", t.replace(hour=0, minute=0, second=0))

# Arithmetic with timedelta
due = d + timedelta(days=30)
print("due:", due, "| days left from Dec 1:", (due - date(2026, 12, 1)).days)
span = datetime(2026, 11, 24, 2, 15) - datetime(2026, 11, 23, 22, 45)
print("span:", span, "=", span.total_seconds(), "seconds")
print("Jan 31 + 30 days:", date(2026, 1, 31) + timedelta(days=30))   # no "months" in timedelta

# Formatting and parsing
print(t.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M"), "|", t.strftime("%d/%m/%Y"), "|", t.strftime("%b %d, %Y at %I:%M %p"))
parsed = datetime.strptime("23/11/2026 18:05", "%d/%m/%Y %H:%M")
print("strptime:", parsed, "| fromisoformat:", datetime.fromisoformat("2026-11-23T18:05:00+00:00"))
try:
    datetime.strptime("2026-13-01", "%Y-%m-%d")
except ValueError as e:
    print("ValueError:", e)

# Comparing and sorting
deadlines = [date(2026, 12, 5), date(2026, 11, 30), date(2027, 1, 2)]
print("earliest:", min(deadlines), "| sorted:", [x.isoformat() for x in sorted(deadlines)])
print("is weekend:", date(2026, 11, 28).weekday() >= 5, "| leap 2028:", date(2028, 2, 29))
EOF
python3 dates.py
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `date` gave the parts and the weekday (**Monday**, ISO weekday 1). `replace()` returned a new datetime at midnight; dates and datetimes are immutable.
- `d + timedelta(days=30)` gave the due date **2026-12-23**, and subtracting dates gave a timedelta whose `.days` is **22**. The overnight span was **3:30:00**, or 12,600 seconds, computed correctly across midnight.
- **Jan 31 + 30 days is March 2**, not "Feb 28": timedelta counts days, not calendar months. For "same day next month" use `dateutil.relativedelta` and decide what happens on the 31st.
- `strftime` formats any way you like (`%I %p` for 12-hour times). `strptime` needs the exact format; month 13 raised **ValueError**, so wrap user input in `try`. `fromisoformat` parsed an ISO string including its `+00:00` offset.
- Dates compare and sort naturally. `weekday() >= 5` detects weekends (Monday is 0), and the calendar knows 2028 is a leap year.

</En>
<Ur>

- `date` ne hisse aur hafte ka din (**Monday**, ISO weekday 1) diya. `replace()` ne aadhi raat wala naya datetime lautaya; dates aur datetimes immutable hain.
- `d + timedelta(days=30)` ne due date **2026-12-23** di, aur dates ghatane se timedelta mila jis ka `.days` **22** hai. Raat bhar ki muddat **3:30:00** yani 12,600 seconds thi, aadhi raat ke paar durust hisaab.
- **Jan 31 + 30 din March 2 hai**, "Feb 28" nahi: timedelta din ginta hai, calendar ke mahine nahi. "Agle mahine usi din" ke liye `dateutil.relativedelta` lein aur faisla karein ke 31 tareekh ko kya ho.
- `strftime` jaise chahein format karta (12 ghante ke liye `%I %p`). `strptime` ko bilkul sahi format chahiye; mahina 13 ne **ValueError** diya, isliye user input `try` me lapetein. `fromisoformat` ne `+00:00` offset samait ISO string parse ki.
- Dates qudrati taur par compare aur sort hoti hain. `weekday() >= 5` weekends pehchanta (Monday 0 hai), aur calendar janta hai ke 2028 leap year hai.

<Step title="Time zones, DST, timestamps and testable expiry checks">

<Tr>
<En>
`zones.py` compares a naive and an aware datetime, converts one UTC meeting time into four zones, shows New York's offset in July and November, adds "one day" across the November DST change two different ways, round-trips a Unix timestamp, and checks token expiry with the current time passed in as a parameter.
</En>
<Ur>
`zones.py` naive aur aware datetime ka muqabla karti, ek UTC meeting ka waqt chaar zones me badalti, July aur November me New York ka offset dikhati, November ki DST tabdeeli ke paar do mukhtalif tareeqon se "ek din" jorti, Unix timestamp aage peeche karti, aur mojooda waqt parameter ke taur par de kar token expiry check karti hai.
</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
cat > zones.py <<'EOF'
from datetime import datetime, timedelta, timezone
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

naive = datetime(2026, 11, 23, 9, 0)                      # no time zone: ambiguous
aware = datetime(2026, 11, 23, 9, 0, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
print("naive tz:", naive.tzinfo, "| aware tz:", aware.tzinfo)
try:
    print(naive < aware)
except TypeError as e:
    print("TypeError:", e)

# Store in UTC, convert for display
meeting = datetime(2026, 11, 23, 15, 0, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
for zone in ["America/New_York", "America/Los_Angeles", "Europe/London", "Asia/Tokyo"]:
    local = meeting.astimezone(ZoneInfo(zone))
    print(f"{zone:<20} {local:%Y-%m-%d %H:%M %Z} (UTC{local:%z})")

# Daylight saving time: the same wall clock time has different UTC offsets
ny = ZoneInfo("America/New_York")
print("July:", datetime(2026, 7, 1, 9, 0, tzinfo=ny).strftime("%H:%M %Z %z"), "| Nov:", datetime(2026, 11, 23, 9, 0, tzinfo=ny).strftime("%H:%M %Z %z"))

# "Add one day" across the DST change (Nov 1, 2026 in New York)
start = datetime(2026, 10, 31, 12, 0, tzinfo=ny)
wall = start + timedelta(days=1)                                    # same local time next day
exact = (start.astimezone(timezone.utc) + timedelta(hours=24)).astimezone(ny)
print("wall clock +1 day:", wall.strftime("%m-%d %H:%M %Z"), "| exactly 24h later:", exact.strftime("%m-%d %H:%M %Z"))

# Unix timestamps are always UTC-based
ts = meeting.timestamp()
print("timestamp:", int(ts), "| back:", datetime.fromtimestamp(ts, tz=timezone.utc).isoformat())

# Token expiry check, with an injected clock so it's testable
def is_expired(expires_at, now):
    return now >= expires_at
issued = datetime(2026, 11, 23, 9, 0, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
expires = issued + timedelta(minutes=15)
for minutes in (14, 15, 16):
    print(f"+{minutes} min expired:", is_expired(expires, issued + timedelta(minutes=minutes)))
print("now() is aware:", datetime.now(timezone.utc).tzinfo is not None)
EOF
python3 zones.py
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- Python **refused to compare** naive and aware datetimes: it can't know which zone the naive one meant. Mixing them is a common source of crashes; make everything aware.
- One stored UTC instant, **15:00 UTC**, displayed as 10:00 in New York, 07:00 in Los Angeles, 15:00 in London and **00:00 the next day** in Tokyo. Store one value, convert per user.
- New York is **-0400 (EDT)** in July and **-0500 (EST)** in November. Hard-coding "UTC-5" would be wrong half the year; `ZoneInfo` applies the rules for you.
- Clocks went back on Nov 1, 2026. Adding `timedelta(days=1)` to an aware local datetime kept the **wall clock** (12:00 the next day), which was really 25 hours later. Adding 24 hours in UTC gave **11:00** local. Choose deliberately: reminders usually want wall-clock time, and expiry windows want exact elapsed time in UTC.
- `timestamp()` gave seconds since 1970 in UTC and `fromtimestamp(ts, tz=timezone.utc)` reversed it. Always pass `tz`, or you get a naive local time.
- `is_expired(expires_at, now)` takes `now` as a parameter, so we tested 14, 15 and 16 minutes exactly, without waiting or patching the clock. In production you pass `datetime.now(timezone.utc)`.

</En>
<Ur>

- Python ne naive aur aware datetimes ka **muqabla karne se inkar** kiya: use pata nahi ke naive wala kis zone ka tha. Inhe milana crashes ka aam sabab hai; sab kuch aware banayen.
- Ek store kiya UTC lamha, **15:00 UTC**, New York me 10:00, Los Angeles me 07:00, London me 15:00 aur Tokyo me **agle din 00:00** dikha. Ek value store karein, har user ke liye badlein.
- New York July me **-0400 (EDT)** aur November me **-0500 (EST)** hai. "UTC-5" hard-code karna aadha saal ghalat hota; `ZoneInfo` aap ke liye qawaid lagata hai.
- Nov 1, 2026 ko ghariyan peeche gayin. Aware local datetime me `timedelta(days=1)` jorne se **ghari ka waqt** (agle din 12:00) qaim raha, jo asal me 25 ghante baad tha. UTC me 24 ghante jorne se local **11:00** mila. Soch kar chunein: reminders aam taur par ghari ka waqt chahte, aur expiry windows UTC me bilkul guzra waqt.
- `timestamp()` ne UTC me 1970 se seconds diye aur `fromtimestamp(ts, tz=timezone.utc)` ne ulta kiya. Hamesha `tz` dein, warna naive local waqt milta hai.
- `is_expired(expires_at, now)` `now` ko parameter ke taur par leta hai, isliye humne 14, 15 aur 16 minute bilkul theek test kiye, intezar ya ghari patch kiye baghair. Production me aap `datetime.now(timezone.utc)` dete hain.

## Common mistakes

<Compare>
<Bad title="Naive local times everywhere">

```text
created = datetime.now()                     # server's local zone, no tzinfo
expires = created + timedelta(hours=1)
db.save(created.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M"))  # which zone? nobody knows later
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Aware UTC, ISO 8601 when stored or sent">

```text
created = datetime.now(timezone.utc)
expires = created + timedelta(hours=1)
db.save(created.isoformat())                 # 2026-11-23T15:00:00+00:00
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Compare>
<Bad title="Fixed offsets instead of zones">

```text
NY = timezone(timedelta(hours=-5))           # wrong during summer (EDT is -4)
local = meeting.astimezone(NY)
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Named IANA zones">

```text
NY = ZoneInfo("America/New_York")            # knows DST and historical changes
local = meeting.astimezone(NY)
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Tr>
<En>

More mistakes to avoid:

- **Mixing up `%m` (month) and `%M` (minute)** in format strings.
- **Assuming months have 30 days** or that a day is always 86,400 seconds in local time.
- **`datetime.utcnow()`**: returns a naive datetime (and is deprecated); use `datetime.now(timezone.utc)`.
- **Using `time.time()` to measure durations**: system clock changes can make it jump; use `time.perf_counter()` or `time.monotonic()`.
- **Parsing dates with ambiguous formats** like `03/04/2026` (March 4 or April 3?); prefer ISO 8601.

</En>
<Ur>

Bachne wali mazeed ghaltiyan:

- **Format strings me `%m` (mahina) aur `%M` (minute) ka ulajhna.**
- **Mahinon ko 30 din ka samajhna** ya ye ke local waqt me din hamesha 86,400 seconds ka hai.
- **`datetime.utcnow()`**: naive datetime lautata (aur deprecated hai); `datetime.now(timezone.utc)` lein.
- **Muddat naapne ke liye `time.time()`**: system ghari ki tabdeeli use uchhal sakti; `time.perf_counter()` ya `time.monotonic()` lein.
- **`03/04/2026` jaise mubham formats wali dates parse karna** (March 4 ya April 3?); ISO 8601 ko tarjeeh dein.

</Ur>
</Tr>

<Tr>
<En>
A meeting is entered as `"2026-12-03 09:30"` in the organizer's zone `America/Chicago`. Parse it, attach the zone, convert it to UTC (print in ISO format) and show it for attendees in `Europe/Berlin` and `Australia/Sydney` as `"Thu 03 Dec 16:30 CET"`-style strings.
</En>
<Ur>
Meeting organizer ke zone `America/Chicago` me `"2026-12-03 09:30"` ke taur par darj hai. Ise parse karein, zone lagayen, UTC me badlein (ISO format me print karein) aur `Europe/Berlin` aur `Australia/Sydney` ke attendees ke liye `"Thu 03 Dec 16:30 CET"` jaisi strings me dikhayen.
</Ur>
</Tr>

<Solution>

<Tr>
<En>

```text
from datetime import datetime, timezone
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

local = datetime.strptime("2026-12-03 09:30", "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M").replace(tzinfo=ZoneInfo("America/Chicago"))
utc = local.astimezone(timezone.utc)
print("UTC:", utc.isoformat())
for zone in ["Europe/Berlin", "Australia/Sydney"]:
    print(f"{zone}: {utc.astimezone(ZoneInfo(zone)):%a %d %b %H:%M %Z}")

# Europe/Berlin: Thu 03 Dec 16:30 CET
# Australia/Sydney: Fri 04 Dec 02:30 AEDT
```

<DeepDive title="More about dates and times">

**Ambiguous and missing local times.** When clocks go back, 01:30 happens twice; `fold=1` picks the second one. When they go forward, 02:30 doesn't exist at all.

**Time zone data.** `zoneinfo` uses the system's IANA database; on Windows or slim containers install the `tzdata` package.

**Monotonic clocks.** `time.monotonic()` and `perf_counter()` never go backwards, which makes them right for timeouts and benchmarks.

**Libraries.** `python-dateutil` (relativedelta, flexible parsing), `pendulum` and `arrow` offer friendlier APIs; databases have `timestamptz` types that store UTC.

**Leap seconds and calendars.** Python ignores leap seconds; non-Gregorian calendars need specialised libraries.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Real world: the double-run billing job">

A billing job was scheduled at "01:30 local time" every night on a server in a zone with DST. On the night clocks went back, 01:30 happened twice and customers were charged twice. On the spring night, 01:30 never happened and nobody was billed.

The fixes:

1. Schedule jobs in UTC.
2. Make billing idempotent: a unique key per customer per billing period so a second run does nothing.
3. Alert when a scheduled job doesn't run.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

- **Token and session expiry** must be compared in UTC with aware datetimes; naive comparisons can extend or shorten validity.
- **Clock skew**: allow a small leeway (seconds) when validating tokens from other servers, but not minutes.
- **Log timestamps in UTC** so incident timelines across servers line up.
- **Parse untrusted dates strictly** and reject out-of-range values (a "valid until 9999" token is suspicious).
- **Certificates expire**: monitor `notAfter` dates and alert weeks before.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="What is the difference between naive and aware datetimes?">
A naive datetime has no time zone information, so the instant it represents is ambiguous; an aware datetime has tzinfo with an offset, so it identifies an exact moment and can be compared and converted correctly.
</QA>

<QA q="Why store timestamps in UTC?">
UTC has no daylight saving changes and is the same everywhere, so stored values are unambiguous, sort correctly and can be converted to any user's local zone for display.
</QA>

<QA q="How do you add one month to a date?">
timedelta has no month unit; use dateutil.relativedelta(months=1) or custom logic, deciding how to handle days like the 31st that don't exist in every month.
</QA>

<QA q="What problems does daylight saving time cause?">
Local times can repeat or not exist, days can be 23 or 25 hours, fixed offsets become wrong for part of the year, and scheduled jobs can run twice or not at all.
</QA>

<QA q="How do you make time-dependent code testable?">
Pass the current time or a clock function as a parameter instead of calling now inside the logic, so tests can supply fixed times.
</QA>

</DeepDive>
