<BigIdea>
<Tr>
<En>
A **regular expression (regex)** is a **pattern** that describes text. Instead of searching for one exact word, you describe a **shape**: "three digits, a dash, three digits, a dash, four digits" is `\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}`. The building blocks are **literals** (`cat`), **character classes** (`[0-9]`, `\d`, `\w`, `\s`, `.`), **quantifiers** for how many (`*`, `+`, `?`, `{3}`), **anchors** for where (`^`, `$`, `\b`), **groups** `( )` to capture parts, and **alternation** `|` for "or". Two security rules: **anchor validation patterns** (`^...$`), and avoid nested quantifiers like `(a+)+` that can freeze a server (**ReDoS**).
</En>
<Ur>
**Regular expression (regex)** text ko bayan karne wala **pattern** hai. Ek bilkul lafz dhoondhne ke bajaye aap ek **shakal** bayan karte hain: "teen hindse, dash, teen hindse, dash, chaar hindse" yani `\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}`. Bunyadi tukre: **literals** (`cat`), **character classes** (`[0-9]`, `\d`, `\w`, `\s`, `.`), kitni dafa ke liye **quantifiers** (`*`, `+`, `?`, `{3}`), kahan ke liye **anchors** (`^`, `$`, `\b`), hisse pakarne ke liye **groups** `( )`, aur "ya" ke liye **alternation** `|`. Do security qawaid: **validation patterns ko anchor karein** (`^...$`), aur `(a+)+` jaise ek ke andar ek quantifiers se bachein jo server jama kar sakte hain (**ReDoS**).
</Ur>
</Tr>

## Learn it step by step

<Walkthrough>

<Step title="Literal matches and the dot">

<Tr>
<En>

Most characters match **themselves**: the pattern `cat` finds "cat". The dot `.` matches **any single character** (except a newline): `gr.y` matches grey and gray. Matching is **case-sensitive** by default, and a pattern matches **anywhere** in the text unless you anchor it.

</En>
<Ur>

Zyada tar huroof **khud se** milte hain: pattern `cat` "cat" dhoondhta hai. Dot `.` **koi bhi ek harf** (nayi line ke siwa) milata hai: `gr.y` grey aur gray dono. Matching default me **case-sensitive** hai, aur pattern text me **kahin bhi** milta hai jab tak anchor na karein.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
printf 'category\ncat\nconcatenate\nCat\ngray\ngrey\ngr8y\n' > words.txt
echo "--- cat:";  grep -E 'cat' words.txt
echo "--- gr.y:"; grep -E 'gr.y' words.txt
```

```text output
--- cat:
category
cat
concatenate
--- gr.y:
gray
grey
gr8y
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>`cat` matched **three** lines: "category", "cat" and "concatenate" all **contain** c-a-t. `Cat` didn't match: case-sensitive. `gr.y` matched gray, grey **and** `gr8y`, because the dot accepts any character, including digits. Often that's more than you want, which is why character classes exist.</En>
<Ur>`cat` ne **teen** lines milayin: "category", "cat" aur "concatenate" sab me c-a-t **maujood** hai. `Cat` nahi mila: case-sensitive. `gr.y` ne gray, grey **aur** `gr8y` milaye, kyun ke dot koi bhi harf, hindse samet, maanta hai. Aksar ye zaroorat se zyada hota hai, isi liye character classes hain.</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Character classes: [...] and shortcuts">

<Tr>
<En>

`[abc]` matches **one** character from the set; `[a-z]` is a range; `[^abc]` (caret first) means **anything except** those. Shortcuts: `\d` digit, `\w` word character (letters, digits, underscore), `\s` whitespace; capitals mean the opposite (`\D` non-digit). In strict POSIX tools use `[0-9]` or `[[:digit:]]`, which work everywhere. Inside `[...]` most metacharacters lose their meaning: `[.]` is a literal dot, and `-` is literal at the start or end (`[-+]`).

</En>
<Ur>

`[abc]` set ka **ek** harf milata hai; `[a-z]` silsila hai; `[^abc]` (pehle caret) ka matlab **in ke siwa koi bhi**. Shortcuts: `\d` hindsa, `\w` word harf (huroof, hindse, underscore), `\s` khaali jagah; bare huroof ulta matlab dete hain (`\D` ghair hindsa). Sakht POSIX tools me `[0-9]` ya `[[:digit:]]` istemal karein, jo har jagah chalte hain. `[...]` ke andar zyada tar metacharacters apna matlab kho dete hain: `[.]` asli dot hai, aur `-` shuru ya aakhir me asli hai (`[-+]`).

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
echo "--- gr[ae]y (only a or e):"; grep -E 'gr[ae]y' words.txt
echo "--- [Cc]at at the start of a line:"; grep -E '^[Cc]at' words.txt
echo 'Order #A17, total $1,500 on 2026-11-10' > order.txt
echo "--- digits [0-9]+:";   grep -oE '[0-9]+' order.txt | tr '\n' ' '; echo
echo "--- non-digits \\D+:"; python3 -c 'import re; print(re.findall(r"\D+", open("order.txt").read().strip()))'
```

```text output
--- gr[ae]y (only a or e):
gray
grey
--- [Cc]at at the start of a line:
category
cat
Cat
--- digits [0-9]+:
17 1 500 2026 11 10 
--- non-digits \D+:
['Order #A', ', total $', ',', ' on ', '-', '-']
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `gr[ae]y` allows only `a` or `e` in the third position, so `gr8y` is gone.
- `^[Cc]at` accepts upper- or lower-case C, but only at the line start, so "concatenate" is out.
- `[0-9]+` found every run of digits. `1,500` was split into `1` and `500` because a comma isn't a digit: patterns do exactly what you write, not what you mean.
- `\D+` (Python) found runs of **non**-digits: the pieces between the numbers.

</En>
<Ur>

- `gr[ae]y` teesri jagah sirf `a` ya `e` maanta hai, isliye `gr8y` nikal gaya.
- `^[Cc]at` bara ya chhota C maanta hai, lekin sirf line ke shuru me, isliye "concatenate" bahar.
- `[0-9]+` ne hindson ka har silsila dhoondha. `1,500` `1` aur `500` me toot gaya kyun ke comma hindsa nahi: patterns wahi karte hain jo aap likhte hain, jo aap ka matlab ho wo nahi.
- `\D+` (Python) ne **ghair** hindson ke silsile dhoondhe: numbers ke beech ke tukre.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Quantifiers: how many">

<Tr>
<En>

Quantifiers say how many times the **previous item** repeats: `*` zero or more, `+` one or more, `?` zero or one (optional), `{3}` exactly 3, `{2,4}` between 2 and 4, `{2,}` 2 or more. A quantifier applies only to the **single** item just before it: `ab+` is "a, then one or more b", not "ab repeated". Use a group for that: `(ab)+`.

</En>
<Ur>

Quantifiers batate hain **pichhli cheez** kitni dafa dohraye: `*` sifar ya zyada, `+` ek ya zyada, `?` sifar ya ek (ikhtiyari), `{3}` bilkul 3, `{2,4}` 2 se 4, `{2,}` 2 ya zyada. Quantifier sirf apne theek pehle wali **ek** cheez par lagta hai: `ab+` ka matlab "a, phir ek ya zyada b", "ab dohrao" nahi. Us ke liye group: `(ab)+`.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
printf 'color\ncolour\ncolouur\n' | grep -E '^colou?r$'
echo 'Call 555-123-4567 or 555-765-4321, not 555-1234 or 55512345678901' > phones.txt
echo "--- \\d{3}-\\d{3}-\\d{4}:"; grep -oE '[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{4}' phones.txt
echo "--- ab+ vs (ab)+:"; echo 'abbb ababab' | grep -oE 'ab+' | tr '\n' ' '; echo '| '"$(echo 'abbb ababab' | grep -oE '(ab)+' | tr '\n' ' ')"
```

```text output
color
colour
--- \d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}:
555-123-4567
555-765-4321
--- ab+ vs (ab)+:
abbb ab ab ab | ab ababab 
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `colou?r` made the `u` optional; `colouur` has two u's, so with `^...$` it fails.
- `[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{4}` found only correctly shaped numbers; `555-1234` is too short and the 14-digit number has no dashes.
- `ab+` found `abbb`, then three separate `ab`s; `(ab)+` found `ab` at the start of `abbb`, then `ababab` as one match.

</En>
<Ur>

- `colou?r` ne `u` ikhtiyari kiya; `colouur` me do u hain, isliye `^...$` ke saath fail.
- `[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{3}-[0-9]{4}` ne sirf sahi shakal wale numbers dhoondhe; `555-1234` chhota hai aur 14 hindson wale me dash nahi.
- `ab+` ne `abbb` dhoondha, phir teen alag `ab`; `(ab)+` ne `abbb` ke shuru me `ab`, phir `ababab` ek match ki tarah.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Anchors and word boundaries">

<Tr>
<En>

Anchors match **positions**, not characters: `^` start, `$` end, `\b` a **word boundary** (between a word character and a non-word character). Without anchors, a pattern matches **anywhere inside** a longer text: fine for searching, dangerous for validation. In Python, `re.fullmatch` checks the whole string, `re.search` finds a match anywhere, and `re.match` only anchors at the start. `grep -w` and `\b` give whole words; `grep -x` whole lines.

</En>
<Ur>

Anchors **jagahen** milate hain, huroof nahi: `^` shuru, `$` aakhir, `\b` **lafz ki hadd** (word harf aur ghair word harf ke beech). Anchors ke baghair pattern lambe text me **kahin bhi** milta hai: dhoondhne ke liye theek, jaanch ke liye khatarnak. Python me `re.fullmatch` poori string check karta hai, `re.search` kahin bhi match dhoondhta hai, aur `re.match` sirf shuru par anchor karta hai. `grep -w` aur `\b` poore alfaaz dete hain; `grep -x` poori lines.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
echo "--- \\bcat\\b (whole word only):"; echo 'cat category concat cat.' | grep -oE '\bcat\b' | wc -l | sed 's/$/ matches/'
python3 - <<'PY'
import re
pattern = r"\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}"
for text in ["555-123-4567", "my number is 555-123-4567", "555-123-4567; DROP TABLE users"]:
    print(f"{text!r:36} search={bool(re.search(pattern, text))!s:5} fullmatch={bool(re.fullmatch(pattern, text))}")
PY
```

```text output
--- \bcat\b (whole word only):
2 matches
'555-123-4567'                       search=True  fullmatch=True
'my number is 555-123-4567'          search=True  fullmatch=False
'555-123-4567; DROP TABLE users'     search=True  fullmatch=False
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>`\bcat\b` matched the standalone `cat` and `cat.` (a dot isn't a word character, so there's a boundary), but not "category" or "concat". `re.search` found the phone pattern **somewhere** in all three strings, so a validator using `search` would accept `555-123-4567; DROP TABLE users`. `re.fullmatch` requires the **entire** string to match, so only the clean number passes. **Always anchor validation patterns.**</En>
<Ur>`\bcat\b` ne akela `cat` aur `cat.` milaya (dot word harf nahi, isliye hadd hai), lekin "category" ya "concat" nahi. `re.search` ne teeno strings me phone pattern **kahin na kahin** paaya, isliye `search` wala validator `555-123-4567; DROP TABLE users` maan leta. `re.fullmatch` **poori** string ka milna maangta hai, isliye sirf saaf number pass hua. **Validation patterns hamesha anchor karein.**</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Groups, alternation and capturing">

<Tr>
<En>

Parentheses `( )` **group** items (so a quantifier or `|` applies to all of them) and **capture** the matched text so you can use it. `|` means "or": `(GET|POST|PUT|DELETE)`. You usually want **parts** of a match: the year from a date, the IP and status from a log line. Groups are numbered by their opening parenthesis from left to right, starting at 1; group 0 is the whole match. `(?:...)` groups **without** capturing.

</En>
<Ur>

Qauseen `( )` cheezon ko **group** karti hain (taa ke quantifier ya `|` sab par lage) aur mile text ko **pakarti** hain taa ke istemal kar saken. `|` ka matlab "ya": `(GET|POST|PUT|DELETE)`. Aam tor par match ke **hisse** chahiye hote hain: tareekh se saal, log line se IP aur status. Groups apni khulne wali qaus ke hisaab se baayen se daayen 1 se number hote hain; group 0 poora match hai. `(?:...)` **pakre baghair** group karta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
python3 - <<'PY'
import re
line = '203.0.113.9 - - [10/Nov/2026:14:02:11 +0000] "POST /api/login HTTP/1.1" 401 532'
m = re.search(r'^(\S+) .*?"(GET|POST|PUT|DELETE) (\S+) [^"]*" (\d{3}) (\d+)$', line)
print("whole match length:", len(m.group(0)))
print("groups:", m.groups())
ip, method, path, status, size = m.groups()
print(f"{ip} sent {method} to {path} -> status {status}")
print("dates:", re.findall(r"(\d{4})-(\d{2})-(\d{2})", "from 2026-11-01 to 2026-11-30"))
PY
```

```text output
whole match length: 79
groups: ('203.0.113.9', 'POST', '/api/login', '401', '532')
203.0.113.9 sent POST to /api/login -> status 401
dates: [('2026', '11', '01'), ('2026', '11', '30')]
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `^(\S+)` captured the client IP (group 1); `.*?"` skipped ahead (lazily) to the first quote.
- `(GET|POST|PUT|DELETE)` captured the method; `(\S+)` the path; `[^"]*"` skipped the rest of the request.
- `(\d{3})` captured the status and `(\d+)$` the size at the end of the line.
- `m.groups()` returned all five values, which we unpacked into variables. `re.findall` with three groups returned year, month, day tuples.

</En>
<Ur>

- `^(\S+)` ne client IP pakra (group 1); `.*?"` (lazy) pehle quote tak aage barha.
- `(GET|POST|PUT|DELETE)` ne method pakra; `(\S+)` ne path; `[^"]*"` ne request ka baqi hissa chhora.
- `(\d{3})` ne status aur `(\d+)$` ne line ke aakhir me size pakra.
- `m.groups()` ne paanchon values lautayin, jo hum ne variables me khol din. Teen groups wale `re.findall` ne saal, mahina, din ke tuples diye.

</Ur>
</Tr>
</Happened>

</Step>

<Step title="Named groups and search-and-replace">

<Tr>
<En>

**Named groups** `(?P<name>...)` (Python) or `(?<name>...)` (JavaScript, PCRE, .NET) label parts so long patterns stay readable. In **replacements**, `\1` / `$1` / `\g<name>` insert captured text: **backreferences**. One replace can reformat thousands of lines (dates, masking, refactoring) with `sed -E`, Python `re.sub`, JavaScript `replace`, or VS Code's regex replace. Preview on a copy first: one wrong pattern changes every line.

</En>
<Ur>

**Named groups** `(?P<name>...)` (Python) ya `(?<name>...)` (JavaScript, PCRE, .NET) hisson par naam lagate hain taa ke lambe patterns parhne me aasaan rahen. **Replacements** me `\1` / `$1` / `\g<name>` pakra hua text daalte hain: **backreferences**. Ek replace hazaron lines ki shakal badal sakta hai (tareekhen, masking, refactoring) `sed -E`, Python `re.sub`, JavaScript `replace` ya VS Code ke regex replace se. Pehle copy par dekh lein: ek ghalat pattern har line badal deta hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
printf 'paid on 03/11/2026\nrefund on 25/12/2026\n' > dates.txt
echo "--- sed: dd/mm/yyyy -> yyyy-mm-dd"
sed -E 's#([0-9]{2})/([0-9]{2})/([0-9]{4})#\3-\2-\1#' dates.txt
python3 - <<'PY'
import re
m = re.search(r"(?P<day>\d{2})/(?P<month>\d{2})/(?P<year>\d{4})", "paid on 03/11/2026")
print("named:", m.group("year"), m.group("month"), m.group("day"))
card = "Card 4111 1111 1111 1234 charged"
```

<Step title="Greedy vs lazy">

<Tr>
<En>

Quantifiers are **greedy**: they match as **much** as possible, then give back only if needed, so `".*"` runs from the first quote to the **last**. Adding `?` makes them **lazy** (`*?`, `+?`): as **little** as possible. Lazy quantifiers exist in Python, JavaScript and PCRE, but **not** in POSIX `grep -E`/`sed -E`. There, use a negated class: `"[^"]*"` ("a quote, any non-quotes, a quote"), which is precise, faster, and works in every flavour.

</En>
<Ur>

Quantifiers **lalchi (greedy)** hote hain: jitna **zyada** ho sake milate hain, phir zaroorat par wapas dete hain, isliye `".*"` pehle quote se **aakhri** tak jata hai. `?` jorne se wo **lazy** (`*?`, `+?`) bante hain: jitna **kam** ho sake. Lazy quantifiers Python, JavaScript aur PCRE me hain, lekin POSIX `grep -E`/`sed -E` me **nahi**. Wahan negated class istemal karein: `"[^"]*"` ("quote, quote ke siwa kuch bhi, quote"), jo durust, tez, aur har flavour me chalti hai.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash

print("negated \"[^\"]*\" :", re.findall(r'"[^"]*"', text))
PY
```


<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>Greedy `".*"` ran to the end of the line and backed up to the **last** quote: one huge match. Lazy `".*?"` stopped at the **nearest** closing quote. `"[^"]*"` can't cross a quote at all, so it gave the same result as lazy, and works in `grep -E` and `sed` too.</En>
<Ur>Greedy `".*"` line ke aakhir tak gaya aur **aakhri** quote tak peeche aaya: ek bohot bara match. Lazy `".*?"` **qareeb tareen** band quote par ruka. `"[^"]*"` quote paar kar hi nahi sakta, isliye lazy jaisa nateeja diya, aur `grep -E` aur `sed` me bhi chalta hai.</Ur>

<Step title="Escaping special characters">

<En>

<Step title="Lookahead: conditions without consuming">

<Tr>
<En>

A **lookahead** `(?=...)` checks that something comes next **without** including it in the match; `(?!...)` checks it does **not**. Lookbehinds `(?<=...)` and `(?<!...)` check what comes before. They express several conditions on the same text ("at least one digit and one uppercase letter", "a price after $"). Supported in Python, JavaScript, PCRE and .NET; **not** in POSIX `grep -E`/`sed`. Each `(?=.*X)` at the start of an anchored pattern acts like an extra rule that must be true somewhere in the string.

</En>
<Ur>

**Lookahead** `(?=...)` check karta hai ke aage kuch aa raha hai, use match me **shamil kiye baghair**; `(?!...)` check karta hai ke **nahi** aa raha. Lookbehinds `(?<=...)` aur `(?<!...)` pehle wala dekhte hain. Ye ek hi text par kai sharten lagate hain ("kam az kam ek hindsa aur ek bara harf", "$ ke baad qeemat"). Python, JavaScript, PCRE aur .NET me hain; POSIX `grep -E`/`sed` me **nahi**. Anchored pattern ke shuru me har `(?=.*X)` ek izafi qaida hai jo string me kahin sach hona chahiye.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
python3 - <<'PY'

for pw in ["password", "Password123", "Chic@go2026xy", "Correct-Horse-9-Battery"]:
    print(f"{pw:24} {'accepted' if rule.match(pw) else 'rejected'}")
print("amounts after $:", re.findall(r"(?<=\$)\d{1,3}(?:,\d{3})*", "Paid $1,500 and $800, tip 50"))
PY
```

<Tr>
<En>

- Four lookaheads each checked the **whole** string: a lowercase, an uppercase, a digit, and a symbol (`[^\w\s]`); then `.{12,}$` required 12+ characters. The first two failed; the other two passed.
- `(?<=\$)` matched only numbers right after `$`, without including it, so the tip `50` was skipped. `\d{1,3}(?:,\d{3})*` ("1–3 digits, then comma + 3 digits any number of times") matched `1,500` whole but not the comma **after** `800`.
- (Modern password advice, e.g. NIST SP 800-63B, prefers **length** and breached-password checks over composition rules, but the regex technique works for any rule set.)

</En>

<Step title="Flavours: grep, sed, Python, JavaScript">

<Tr>
<En>

Regex syntax differs between tools, so a pattern copied from Stack Overflow may silently mean something else in yours. <Step title="Security: unanchored validation, ReDoS and regex injection">

<Tr>
<En>

Three classic regex security bugs. **(1)** Validation without `^...$` accepts dangerous input around a valid-looking part. **(2) ReDoS:** nested or overlapping quantifiers like `(a+)+$` take **exponential** time on certain inputs; one request can block a Node.js server's single thread for everyone (real outages: Stack Overflow 2016, Cloudflare July 2019). **(3) Regex injection:** building a pattern from user input lets users change its meaning. Defences: avoid nested quantifiers, limit input length, use linear-time engines for untrusted input (Go RE2, Rust `regex`, the `re2` bindings, .NET `NonBacktracking`), set timeouts, and lint with `eslint-plugin-regexp` or `safe-regex`.

</En>
<Ur>

Regex ke teen mashhoor security bugs. **(1)** `^...$` ke baghair jaanch sahi lagne wale hisse ke ird gird khatarnak input maan leti hai. **(2) ReDoS:** `(a+)+$` jaise ek ke andar ek ya takrate quantifiers kuch inputs par **exponential** waqt lete hain; ek request Node.js server ki akeli thread sab ke liye rok sakti hai (asli outages: Stack Overflow 2016, Cloudflare July 2019). **(3) Regex injection:** user ke input se pattern banana user ko us ka matlab badalne deta hai. Bachao: nested quantifiers se bachein, input ki lambai mehdood karein, na-bharose wale input ke liye linear waqt engines (Go RE2, Rust `regex`, `re2` bindings, .NET `NonBacktracking`), timeouts lagayen, aur `eslint-plugin-regexp` ya `safe-regex` se lint karein.

</Ur>
</Tr>

```bash
python3 - <<'PY'
import re, time
vulnerable = re.compile(r"^(a+)+$")      # nested quantifier
safe = re.compile(r"^a+$")              # same meaning, no nesting
for n in (10, 25):
    text = "a" * n + "!"                 # almost matches, then fails at the end
    start = time.perf_counter(); vulnerable.match(text); slow = time.perf_counter() - start
    start = time.perf_counter(); safe.match(text); fast = time.perf_counter() - start
    print(f"n={n:2}: vulnerable took over 0.2s? {slow > 0.2!s:5} | safe took over 0.2s? {fast > 0.2}")

user_search = ".*"
names = ["sam", "emma", "jack"]
print("unescaped user pattern matches:", [x for x in names if re.search(user_search, x)])
print("escaped user pattern matches:  ", [x for x in names if re.search(re.escape(user_search), x)])
PY
```

```text output
n=10: vulnerable took over 0.2s? False | safe took over 0.2s? False
n=25: vulnerable took over 0.2s? True  | safe took over 0.2s? False
unescaped user pattern matches: ['sam', 'emma', 'jack']
escaped user pattern matches:   []
```

<Happened>
<Tr>
<En>

- `^(a+)+$` on a's followed by `!` must fail at the end, and the backtracking engine tries **every way** to split the a's into groups first: about 2ⁿ attempts. Each extra `a` doubles the time; at n=25 it took well over a second, and n=40 would take days.
- `^a+$` describes the same strings with no nesting and fails instantly at any length.
- A search box that passes user input straight to `re.search` lets someone type `.*` and match **everything** (or a ReDoS pattern to freeze the server). `re.escape` turned the input into a literal search.

</En>
<Ur>

- `!` ke baad wale a's par `^(a+)+$` ko aakhir me fail hona hai, aur backtracking engine pehle a's ko groups me baantne ka **har tareeqa** aazmata hai: takreeban 2ⁿ koshishen. Har izafi `a` waqt dugna karta hai; n=25 par ek second se kahin zyada laga, aur n=40 par din lag jate.

## Common mistakes

<Compare>
<Bad title="Unanchored, unescaped, nested">

```text
re.search(r"\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}", value)        # accepts "555-123-4567; DROP TABLE"
re.search(r"example.com", host)               # also matches "exampleXcom"
re.match(r"^([a-z]+\.?)+@example\.com$", s)   # nested quantifier: ReDoS
```

</Bad>
<Good title="Full match, escaped, simple">

```text
re.fullmatch(r"\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}", value)
re.fullmatch(r"(?:[a-z0-9-]+\.)*example\.com", host)
re.fullmatch(r"[^@\s]+@example\.com", s[:254])
```

</Good>
</Compare>

<Tr>
<En>

More mistakes to avoid:

- **Greedy `.*`** swallowing too much; use `[^"]*` or lazy `.*?`.
- **Forgetting the `g` flag** in JavaScript `replace`.
- **Wrong flavour:** `\d`, lookarounds or lazy quantifiers where they aren't supported; `+` and `|` in BRE.
- **Non-raw strings** in Python (`"\d"` instead of `r"\d"`), or double escaping in JSON/YAML configs.
- **Parsing HTML/JSON/CSV with regex** instead of a real parser.
- **Over-strict validation** that rejects real data (names like O'Brien, `+` in emails, new phone formats).
- **No tests:** every pattern needs strings that must and must not match.

</En>
<Ur>

Bachne wali mazeed ghaltiyan:

- **Greedy `.*`** zaroorat se zyada nigal jata hai; `[^"]*` ya lazy `.*?` istemal karein.
- JavaScript `replace` me **`g` flag bhoolna**.
- **Ghalat flavour:** jahan support nahi wahan `\d`, lookarounds ya lazy quantifiers; BRE me `+` aur `|`.
- Python me **raw strings ke baghair** (`r"\d"` ke bajaye `"\d"`), ya JSON/YAML configs me dohra escaping.
- **HTML/JSON/CSV ko regex se parse karna** asli parser ke bajaye.
- **Had se zyada sakht jaanch** jo asli data radd kare (O'Brien jaise naam, emails me `+`, naye phone formats).
- **Tests nahi:** har pattern ke liye wo strings jo milni chahiyen aur jo nahi.

</Ur>
</Tr>

## Remember

<Takeaways>
<Tr>
<En>

- A regex describes a **shape** of text: literals, classes, quantifiers, anchors, groups, `|`.
- Quantifiers apply to the single previous item; group with `( )`.
- Anchor every validation pattern (`^...$`, `fullmatch`).
- Groups capture parts; named groups and backreferences power search-and-replace.
- Greedy vs lazy: prefer `[^"]*`, which works everywhere.
- Escape metacharacters and **always** escape user input.
- Know your flavour: use `grep -E`/`sed -E`; `-P` for `\d` and lookarounds.
- Avoid nested quantifiers (ReDoS); limit input; use parsers for structured formats.

</En>

<DeepDive title="Real world: one email check froze the whole API">

A Node.js API validated email addresses with a pattern copied from the internet: `^([a-zA-Z0-9]+\.?)+@example\.com$`. Its nested quantifier looked harmless. One day a single request with a 40-character "email" of letters followed by `!` pinned the CPU at 100%, and because Node.js runs JavaScript on one thread, **every** user's request hung until the process restarted. The team found it with a CPU profile, replaced the pattern with `^[^@\s]+@example\.com$`, limited input to 254 characters, added `eslint-plugin-regexp` to CI to catch super-linear patterns, and switched user-facing search to the `re2` package. Separately, their log-alerting rules were rewritten with named groups and unit tests, after a missing `^` anchor let a crafted user-agent string trigger fake "admin login" alerts.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Security lens">

Regexes are security controls in validators, WAFs, log alerts and secret scanners. A wrong anchor or a slow pattern turns a control into a vulnerability.

- **Anchor and fully match** every validation pattern; validate on the **server**, not only in the browser.
- **Allow-list, don't block-list:** describe valid input instead of listing every bad pattern; attackers find variants (encodings, case changes, extra spaces).
- **ReDoS:** avoid nested and overlapping quantifiers, limit input length, use RE2 or timeouts for untrusted input, run regex linters in CI.
- **Regex injection:** escape user input before putting it into patterns, including MongoDB `$regex` queries.
- **Detection rules** (SIEM, IDS, WAF, gitleaks): test them against real attack samples **and** normal traffic to measure false positives and negatives.
- **Log injection:** attackers can put fake log lines in user agents or usernames (newlines, ANSI codes). Anchor log-parsing patterns and strip control characters.

</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Interview questions">

<QA q="What is the difference between greedy and lazy quantifiers?">
Greedy quantifiers such as star and plus match as much text as possible and then backtrack if needed, so a pattern like quote dot-star quote spans from the first to the last quote. Lazy versions written with a trailing question mark match as little as possible, stopping at the first possible end. A negated character class, such as a quote followed by non-quote characters and a quote, is often an even better alternative.
</QA>

<QA q="Why must validation patterns be anchored?">
Without anchors a regex search succeeds if any part of the input matches, so input such as a valid phone number followed by an SQL payload would pass validation. Anchoring with caret and dollar, or using a full-match function, ensures the entire input conforms to the pattern.
</QA>

<QA q="What is ReDoS and how do you prevent it?">
Regular expression denial of service happens when a backtracking regex engine takes exponential or polynomial time on crafted input, usually because of nested or overlapping quantifiers such as a plus inside a group with another plus. It can block a server's CPU. Prevent it by avoiding such patterns, limiting input length, using linear-time engines like RE2 for untrusted input, setting match timeouts, and linting patterns in CI.
</QA>

<QA q="What do capturing groups and backreferences do?">
Parentheses group part of a pattern and capture the text it matched, numbered from left to right or named. The captured text can be extracted in code and reused in replacements with backreferences such as dollar one or backslash one, for example to reorder the parts of a date.
</QA>

<QA q="What is the difference between grep, grep -E and grep -P?">
Plain grep uses POSIX basic regular expressions, where characters such as plus, question mark, pipe and parentheses are literal unless escaped. grep -E uses extended regular expressions, where they are operators. grep -P uses Perl-compatible regular expressions, which add features such as backslash-d, lazy quantifiers and lookarounds.
</QA>

</DeepDive>
